Convective and Radiative Heat Transfer: The Quantitative Puzzle of the Three Modes

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the heat conduction lesson; the concept of thermal radiation. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Beyond conduction there are convection and radiation: convection uses the film coefficient h to summarize the complex mechanisms by which flow carries heat, and radiation grows nonlinearly as T⁴ — the three in parallel make up the basic budget of engineering heat exchange.

Newton's law of cooling q=hAΔT; radiation P=εσA·T⁴

From assumptions to results

  1. Convection couples energy conservation to fluid motion; in engineering, h is given by empirical/dimensional correlations (tens to over a thousand W/(m²K) for forced convection, 2–25 for natural convection).
  2. Radiation is given by the Stefan–Boltzmann law; a body's net radiation is εσA(T⁴−T_surr⁴).
  3. The three channels act in parallel on the same surface; the thermal conductances add.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point out the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Worked example and check

For a human body (about 1.8 m², ε≈0.97, skin at 33 °C, surrounding walls at 20 °C): radiation is about 130 W and natural convection about 60–90 W — clothing and wind speed shift the ratio between the two channels; Earth's 288 K effective radiative balance is the energy ledger of the greenhouse lesson in field 14.

Range of applicability

h depends on geometry and flow regime (Nusselt-number correlations); radiative view factors and gas emissivity cannot be neglected in high-precision budgets.

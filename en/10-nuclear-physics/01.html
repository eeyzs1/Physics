Half-life is a statistical law

Lesson goal: be able to explain "half-life is a statistical law" in your own words, and complete the calculations and validity-condition judgments below on your own.

Prerequisites: quantum mechanics; basics of relativity. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

Start with a question

Starting from 1000 nuclei, how many are expected to remain after 2 half-lives? Note your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understand the model

For a large collection of identical unstable nuclei, when each nucleus decays with the same, independent probability per unit time, the number of undecayed nuclei follows exponential decay. The half-life describes the aggregate law; it cannot predict the exact decay time of any individual nucleus.

N(t) = N₀·2^(−t/T)

Order of use: identify the system and conditions → make the units consistent → write the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The precise meanings and conventions of the variables are given in the example below.

Work through the example with reasoning

Starting with 800 nuclei, after 3 half-lives the expected number remaining is 100. Small samples show statistical fluctuations, so the result need not equal the expectation exactly.

Check the boundary cases

The half-life is not the time needed for all nuclei to decay.

Look back at the example: name at least one neglected factor and explain under what circumstances it could affect the result. After changing one condition, does the original formula still apply?

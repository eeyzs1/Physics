Nuclear reaction thresholds and momentum conservation

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: four-momentum; relativistic energy. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in the same field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

An endothermic reaction must not only make up the rest-mass difference but also satisfy momentum conservation. In a fixed-target experiment the bulk motion of the final state carries off energy, so the beam threshold is usually higher than the simple negative of the Q value.

E_a,th=[M_f²−m_a²−m_b²]c²/(2m_b)

From assumptions to results

  1. With target b at rest, the invariant is m_a²c⁴+m_b²c⁴+2m_bc²E_a.
  2. At threshold the final state has no relative kinetic energy in the center-of-momentum frame and equals M_f²c⁴.
  3. Solve for the incident total energy.

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Using c=1, m_a=m_b=1, and total final rest mass 3, the incident total-energy threshold is (9−1−1)/2=3.5; the beam kinetic energy is 2.5.

Range of applicability

Target at rest and the minimum allowed final-state mass known; selection rules may still forbid a process that is kinematically allowed.

Independent calculation

Show solution

Total energy 3.5 minus incident rest energy 1 gives 2.5.

Transfer and counterexample

Why does an electron–positron collider usually use the total energy more efficiently than electrons striking a fixed target?

Check the transfer exercise solution

A symmetric collision can make the total momentum nearly zero, so more beam energy goes into producing new particles in the center-of-momentum frame rather than into bulk translational motion.

Advanced self-test

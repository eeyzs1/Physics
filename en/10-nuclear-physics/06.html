Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Rutherford scattering and the discovery of the nucleus

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: Coulomb's law; the lesson on cross sections. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in the same field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

The angular distribution of α particles scattered by the Coulomb potential of a point charge goes as ∝1/sin⁴(θ/2): the large-angle scattering rate far exceeded the "plum pudding" model, and in 1911 Rutherford proposed the atomic nucleus on this basis — the prototype of the scattering experiment used as a "microscope".

dσ/dΩ=(Z₁Z₂e²/16πε₀E)²·1/sin⁴(θ/2)

From assumptions to results

  1. The Coulomb orbit of a point charge is a hyperbola; the Binet equation gives the relation between the deflection angle and the impact parameter, tan(θ/2)=k/(2Eb).
  2. Differentiating with respect to the impact parameter gives the angular distribution ∝1/sin⁴(θ/2).
  3. Comparison with the large-angle counts from gold foil leads to a nuclear radius below 10⁻¹⁴ m.

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Scattering of a 5 MeV α particle off gold: the cross-section ratio between θ=90° and θ=30° is (1/0.5)⁴=16, and compared with θ=10° it differs by over a thousandfold — large angles are rare but decisively support the nuclear model of the atom (link to the history-of-science lesson).

Range of applicability

Point-charge and single-scattering approximation; the high-energy nuclear-force regime and electron screening require corrections.

Independent calculation

Show solution

(sin90/sin30)⁴=2⁴=16.

Transfer and counterexample

Why are large-angle scattering events rare yet the most decisive for distinguishing models?

Check the transfer exercise solution

A uniformly charged positive sphere hardly produces large angles; only the close-range Coulomb field of a compact nucleus gives the 1/sin⁴ tail — rare events are the discriminating evidence.

Advanced self-test

This one takes an extra step or requires a unit conversion; switch to it when reviewing on days 1, 3, and 7 to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

∝1/sin⁴(θ/2): predominantly forward, large angles rare.

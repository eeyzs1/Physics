Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Nuclear astrophysics: from the pp chain to the s and r processes

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the lesson on binding energy; the lesson on stellar structure. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in the same field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

The energy of stars and the elements of the universe both come from nuclear reactions: on the main sequence the pp chain/CNO burns hydrogen into helium; elements heavier than iron are synthesized by the s process (slow neutron capture) and the r process (rapid capture in explosive events) — the double peak above the iron peak in the chart of nuclides is the fingerprint of these two routes.

pp-chain net reaction 4p→⁴He+2e⁺+2ν (26.7 MeV)

From assumptions to results

  1. At the solar-core temperature of 1.5×10⁷ K, the pp chain burns slowly, bottlenecked by the weak process p+p→d+e⁺+ν (stable for about ten billion years).
  2. Massive stars later ignite He, C, and O up to Fe (the binding-energy peak).
  3. Fusing iron absorbs energy; the neutron flux of a core-collapse explosion synthesizes r-process heavy elements within seconds, while the slow s process in AGB stars builds the other branch.
  4. The abundance double peak and kilonova observations confirm this picture.

Example and check

Each second the Sun fuses about 6×10¹¹ kg of hydrogen into helium (about 4×10⁹ kg of mass converted into energy); abundance differences of Eu (an r-process marker) and Ba (s process) in different stars record the chemical evolution of the Galaxy; the kilonova from a neutron-star merger (observed in 2017 after GW170817) confirmed that the r process makes gold.

Transfer and counterexample

Why are elements heavier than iron synthesized mainly in explosive or merger environments?

Check the transfer exercise solution

The iron peak has the highest binding energy per nucleon, so fusion no longer releases energy; neutron capture and explosive energies are needed to force the synthesis — steady burning stops at iron.

Magic numbers and the shell model

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: binding energy; angular momentum and spin. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in the same field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

Nucleons fill shells the way electrons do, but strong spin–orbit coupling rewrites the magic-number sequence as 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126; nuclei at the magic numbers are exceptionally stable — the "noble gases" among nuclei.

Magic numbers 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126

From assumptions to results

  1. A three-dimensional harmonic oscillator plus a flat-bottomed well gives 2, 8, 20 and then stops.
  2. Introducing strong spin–orbit coupling splits the levels widely and restacks the gaps, producing all six magic numbers, in agreement with the sharp peaks in nucleon separation energies.

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Lead-208 (Z=82, N=126) is doubly magic and exceptionally stable; tin, Z=50, has ten stable isotopes — direct evidence for the magic numbers.

Range of applicability

Mean-field approximation; deformed nuclei are handled with the Nilsson model; weakly bound nuclei near the drip lines need new frameworks.

Transfer and counterexample

Why does tin have especially many stable isotopes?

Check the transfer exercise solution

The closed shell at Z=50 is insensitive to changes in neutron number, so the binding-energy curve is locally flat.

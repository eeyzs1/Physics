Every measurement has an uncertainty

Lesson goal: be able to explain "every measurement has an uncertainty" in your own words, and independently complete the calculations and conditions-of-validity judgments below.

Prerequisites: Learning Preparation; basics of probability and statistics. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

Think about one question first

s = 3 mm, N = 9: about how many mm is the standard error of the mean? Note your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understand the model

Repeated measurements let you estimate random fluctuations, while calibration is used to identify systematic bias. Under independent, identically distributed conditions the standard error of the mean decreases with the square root of the sample size; repeating the same biased measurement does not automatically remove the bias.

u_mean = s/√N

Order of use: identify the object of study and the conditions → make the units consistent → write the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The precise meanings and conventions of the variables are given in the worked example below.

Work through the example with reasoning

The standard deviation of a single measurement is 2 mm; with 16 independent measurements, the standard error of the mean is about 0.5 mm. If the ruler's zero point is off by 1 mm, that offset is not averaged away.

Check the boundary cases

High precision does not guarantee high accuracy; reporting many decimal places does not mean a small uncertainty.

Look back at the example: name at least one factor that was neglected, and explain under what circumstances it could affect the result. After changing one condition, does the original formula still apply?

Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Noise, signal-to-noise ratio, and lock-in amplification

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: uncertainty; signals. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this same field do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

Thermal noise and 1/f noise limit the measurement of weak signals; lock-in amplification first modulates the signal to a known frequency, then uses an extremely narrow equivalent bandwidth to improve the signal-to-noise ratio by several orders of magnitude.

SNR∝signal/√B; single-pole RC output filter equivalent noise bandwidth B=1/(4τ)

From assumptions to results

  1. White noise has a flat spectral density, and the noise power is proportional to the bandwidth.
  2. Modulation shifts the signal to a clean frequency band; mixing plus a low-pass filter compresses the equivalent noise bandwidth to 1/(4τ), the noise shrinks as √B, and the signal-to-noise ratio grows as √τ.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and checks

A lock-in with a time constant of 1 s has an equivalent noise bandwidth of 0.25 Hz; compared with a 10 kHz direct measurement, the signal-to-noise ratio improves by a factor of √(10⁴/0.25)=200.

Limits of validity

This assumes white noise dominates; under long integration, drift and nonlinearity appear.

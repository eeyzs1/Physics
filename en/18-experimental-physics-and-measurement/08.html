Small samples and the t distribution

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the uncertainty lesson; the Poisson lesson. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this same field do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

With few samples, the estimate of the standard deviation is itself uncertain, so the t distribution must be used to widen the confidence interval: t=(x̄−μ)/(s/√n), with degrees of freedom ν=n−1; ignoring the t correction systematically underestimates small-sample errors — a common fatal flaw in lab reports.

t=(x̄−μ)/(s/√n), ν=n−1

From assumptions to results

  1. When σ is unknown, the standardized sample mean no longer follows a Gaussian but t_ν.
  2. For small ν the tails are fatter, and the 95% confidence coefficient grows from 1.96 (2.57 at ν=5, 2.23 at ν=10).
  3. As ν→∞ it returns to the Gaussian.
  4. The Welch correction handles the comparison of two groups with unequal variances.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and checks

Δ=1.0, s=2.0, n=16: t=2.0, ν=15, critical value 2.13 — just short of significance; with the same data but n=6 (ν=5) the critical value is 2.57, even less significant.

Limits of validity

The t test assumes approximate normality; for strongly skewed data use nonparametric or bootstrap methods.

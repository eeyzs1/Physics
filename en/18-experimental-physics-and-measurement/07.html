Quantifying thermal noise and shot noise

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the uncertainty lesson; quantum concepts. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this same field do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

The thermal motion of the charge carriers in a resistor gives a voltage noise v_n=√(4kTR·Δf), and the discreteness of the current gives shot noise i_n=√(2eI·Δf) — these two formulas set the noise floor of electronic measurements.

Johnson: v_n=√(4kTR·Δf); shot: i_n=√(2eI·Δf)

From assumptions to results

  1. Nyquist derived the thermal-noise spectral density 4kTR from the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (white-noise approximation; the quantum correction matters at high frequency).
  2. Shot noise comes from the quantization of charge: for Poisson arrivals the current fluctuation variance is 2eIΔf.
  3. Both grow with the square root of the bandwidth — narrowing the band is the basic strategy for measuring weak signals (links to the lock-in lesson).

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and checks

R=1 kΩ, T=300 K, Δf=10 kHz: v_n≈0.41 μV; for I=1 mA and Δf=1 MHz the shot noise is about 18 nA; good low-noise design computes these two floors first, then chooses components.

Limits of validity

Classical white-noise approximation (hν≪kT); 1/f noise must be added separately at low frequencies.

Independent calculation

Show solution

√(4kTRΔf)=√(1.66×10⁻¹³)≈4.1×10⁻⁷ V.

Transfer and counterexample

Why does a narrower measurement bandwidth give a better signal-to-noise ratio?

Compare with the transfer solution

Both noise powers are ∝Δf; narrowing the band pushes the noise down while the signal power is preserved.

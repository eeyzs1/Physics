Polarization and Malus's law

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: interference and superposition of light; vector decomposition. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Polarization describes the direction of the electric-field oscillation. When linearly polarized light passes through a polarizer, the transmitted intensity varies with the square of the cosine of the angle, following Malus's law; reflection, scattering, and certain crystals can all alter or analyze polarization.

I=I₀cos²θ

From assumptions to results

  1. Decompose the incident electric field into components parallel and perpendicular to the polarizer's transmission axis.
  2. Only the parallel component passes through.
  3. Intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude, giving cos²θ.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

At θ=60° the transmission is I₀/4; two crossed polarizers on their own transmit no light, but inserting a third at 45° between them gives a transmission of I₀/8.

Range of applicability

An ideal polarizer and monochromatic linearly polarized light; circular and elliptical polarization require Jones vectors or Stokes parameters for their description.

Independent calculation

Show solution

cos²45°=0.5.

Transfer exercise and counterexample

If a third polarizer at 45° is inserted between two crossed polarizers, what is the transmission?

Compare with the transfer-exercise solution

I₀cos²45°·cos²45°=I₀/8.

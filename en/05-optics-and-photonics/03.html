Diffraction and imaging resolution

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: wave superposition; small-angle approximations. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Waves emitted from different positions across the aperture superpose coherently in the far field. A finite aperture keeps a point source from being imaged as an infinitely small point; resolution is therefore tied to both the wavelength and the aperture.

Single-slit intensity I=I₀(sinβ/β)², β=πa·sinθ/λ; circular-aperture Rayleigh criterion θ_R≈1.22λ/D

From assumptions to results

  1. The Fraunhofer approximation links the Fourier transform of the aperture field to the far field.
  2. A uniform circular aperture produces an Airy pattern.
  3. The first zero gives the factor 1.22.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

For λ=500 nm and D=0.1 m, θ_R≈6.1×10⁻⁶ rad. Enlarging the aperture improves this ideal diffraction limit.

Range of applicability

An ideal circular aperture, appropriate imaging conditions, and the Rayleigh convention; aberrations, noise, and sampling also limit the actual resolving power.

Independent calculation

Show solution

θ_R is inversely proportional to D, so it becomes 1/2 of its original value.

Transfer exercise and counterexample

Why doesn't making the pixels smaller always let you resolve two points that are closer together?

Compare with the transfer-exercise solution

If the optical transfer has already lost the relevant spatial frequencies, increasing the sampling density alone will not recover the information, and it is also limited by the signal-to-noise ratio.

Advanced topic · About 30–60 min

Fermat's principle, prisms, and aberrations

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the geometric optics lesson; the concept of the variational method. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Light travels along paths where the optical path is stationary (Fermat's principle), and the law of refraction is its direct corollary; a prism at minimum deviation gives the classic measurement of the refractive index; aberrations (spherical, chromatic, coma) set the limits of real lenses.

Stationary optical path; n=sin[(A+δ_m)/2]/sin(A/2)

From assumptions to results

  1. Taking the variation of the optical path gives Snell's law (structurally identical to the stationary action in mechanics).
  2. The symmetric path gives the minimum-deviation condition, and the geometry leads to the prism formula.
  3. Spherical aberration arises because marginal rays have a shorter focal length; chromatic aberration arises from the dispersion of n(λ) (quantified by the Abbe number); a cemented doublet uses complementary positive and negative dispersions to cancel chromatic aberration.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

For a prism with apex angle 60° and n=1.5: sin((60+δ_m)/2)=0.75, δ_m≈37°; crown glass has an Abbe number of about 64 and flint about 35 — the classic combination for an achromatic doublet.

Range of applicability

An ideal thin-prism formula; strong dispersion and large apex angles require wavelength-by-wavelength ray tracing.

Independent calculation

Show solution

(60+δ_m)/2=arcsin0.75≈48.6°, δ_m≈37°.

Transfer exercise and counterexample

Is the similarity between Fermat's principle and the principle of stationary action a coincidence?

Compare with the transfer-exercise solution

No — both are the short-wavelength limit of a stationary wave/quantum phase; the mathematical structures share a common origin.

Advanced self-test

This one takes one more step than the previous problem, or requires a unit conversion; switch to this problem when reviewing on days 1, 3, and 7, to avoid memorizing answers.

Show solution

Positive and negative dispersions complement each other, canceling the drift of the focal length with wavelength.

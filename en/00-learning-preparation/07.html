Probability, Expectation, and Fluctuations

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: basic counting; square roots. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

A random variable is described by a distribution: the expectation is the long-run average, and the variance measures fluctuations. Variances of independent random variables add; a Poisson count has variance equal to its mean, which is the origin of √N noise in experiments.

〈X〉=ΣxP(x); σ²=〈X²〉−〈X〉²; Poisson: σ²=〈N〉

From assumptions to results

  1. Expand the definitions to get variance=〈X²〉−〈X〉².
  2. When independent, the covariance is zero and the variances add directly.
  3. Substituting the Poisson distribution gives variance equal to the mean.

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

With an average of 100 counts per second, σ=10 and the relative fluctuation is 10%; accumulating counts to 10000 gives σ=100, and the relative fluctuation drops to 1%. The signal-to-noise ratio improves as √N.

Limits of validity

When independence is broken (dead time, correlated events), the √N rule fails; systematic bias is not removed by averaging.

Independent calculation

Show solution

σ=√400=20.

Transfer and counterexample

Two independent measurements each have variance σ²; what is the standard deviation of their average?

Check the transfer-exercise solution

The average has variance σ²/4, so the standard deviation is σ/2.

Advanced self-test

This one takes an extra step or requires a unit conversion; on review days 1, 3, and 7, do this problem instead to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

σ/⟨N⟩=30/900=3.33%.

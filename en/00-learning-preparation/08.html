Taylor Expansion and Order-of-Magnitude Estimates

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: derivatives. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

A smooth function can be approximated by a polynomial near a point. Physics derivations often use the leading term to capture the main behavior and the next term to estimate the error; how many orders to keep is decided by the accuracy you need.

f(x+δ)=f(x)+f′δ+½f″δ²+…

From assumptions to results

  1. Match the function's values and derivatives order by order with a polynomial; this fixes the coefficients uniquely.
  2. The remainder is of the order of the first discarded term.

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

sinθ≈θ at θ=30°≈0.524 rad has true value 0.5, an error of about 4.7%; this is why a small-angle pendulum is approximately harmonic, with a correction of order θ³/6.

Limits of validity

Reliable only within the radius of convergence and over small intervals; near singularities a low-order truncation can seriously mislead.

Independent calculation

Show solution

1−(0.1)²/2=1−0.005=0.995.

Transfer and counterexample

When using (1+x)²≈1+2x with x=0.01, how large is the neglected term?

Check the transfer-exercise solution

x²=1×10⁻⁴, i.e. of order 0.01%.

Advanced self-test

This one takes an extra step or requires a unit conversion; on review days 1, 3, and 7, do this problem instead to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

The absolute error is about θ³/6=0.00133. Divide by sin(0.2)≈0.19867 to get 0.67%. The leading relative error is θ²/6, not θ³/6.

Before moving on to the next stage

  1. Plug a numerical case you invent yourself into this lesson's formulas; estimate the order of magnitude first, then compute exactly.
  2. Explain how the conclusion would change if the strongest assumption in the derivation were removed.
  3. In your next review session, do the advanced problem before the basic one, and compare the time spent and error causes of the two attempts.

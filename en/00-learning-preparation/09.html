Trigonometric Identities, Complex Exponentials, and Series Sums

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the complex-phase lesson; the derivatives lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

Trigonometry in physics goes far beyond "looking things up in a table": Euler's formula turns sum-to-product transformations into multiplication of exponentials, and the commonly used series and identities are the muscle memory of derivations — from interference phases to Fourier coefficients, they must be ready at hand.

e^(iθ) representation; sin(A±B), double- and half-angle formulas

From assumptions to results

  1. Write sin and cos as linear combinations of complex exponentials, and the angle-addition formulas become the single line e^(iA)e^(iB).
  2. Double-/half-angle formulas follow from squaring and taking square roots.
  3. The error orders of the small-angle approximations sinθ≈θ and cosθ≈1−θ²/2 can be read straight off the Taylor series.
  4. The geometric series and the expansion of 1/(1−x) reappear throughout perturbation theory and summation.

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

sin75°=sin(45°+30°)=sin45cos30+cos45sin30≈0.966; the resultant amplitude 2|cos(Δφ/2)| of two waves with phase difference Δφ comes directly from the sum-to-product identities; the simplification of ∫sin²=θ/2−sin2θ/4 relies entirely on the double-angle formula.

Limits of validity

Angles are written in radians; keep as many orders as the required numerical precision demands.

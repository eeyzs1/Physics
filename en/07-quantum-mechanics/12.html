Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Angular momentum coupling and Clebsch–Gordan coefficients

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the angular momentum lesson; the identical particles lesson. If the prerequisites are shaky, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The combination of two angular momenta is again an angular momentum: j runs from |j₁−j₂| to j₁+j₂, and each j has 2j+1 components; the coupled and uncoupled bases are related by Clebsch–Gordan coefficients — spectral terms, spin singlet/triplet states, and hyperfine structure are all written with them.

|jm⟩=ΣC(j₁j₂j;m₁m₂m)|j₁m₁⟩|j₂m₂⟩

From postulates to results

  1. The total angular momentum J=J₁+J₂ commutes with J² and J_z.
  2. Starting from the highest-weight state, apply lowering operators repeatedly to generate the j=j₁+j₂ multiplet; orthogonality supplies the lower j.
  3. Two spin-½ particles combine into j=1 (triplet) and j=0 (singlet), with the CG coefficients written out explicitly.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Worked example and check

Coupling ℓ=1 with s=½: j=3/2 (4-fold) and j=1/2 (2-fold); the sodium D doublet comes from the difference between the j=1/2 and j=3/2 transitions from 3p to 3s (the link to the AMO field).

Range of applicability

LS coupling applies when the electrostatic interaction exceeds the spin–orbit one; heavy atoms cross over to jj coupling.

Independent calculation

Show solution

2j+1=4.

Transfer and counterexample

Why do helium's singlet and triplet states differ in energy while sharing the same CG structure?

Check your solution to the transfer problem

The spatial symmetry and the spin coupling pair oppositely, and the exchange energy produces the splitting — the same mathematics, a different physical origin.

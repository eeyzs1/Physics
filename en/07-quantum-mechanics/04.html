The Schrödinger equation and time evolution

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: probability amplitudes; energy levels. If the prerequisites are shaky, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The evolution of the wave function is generated by the Hamiltonian operator and is deterministic, linear, and reversible; randomness enters only at measurement. An energy eigenstate acquires only an overall phase, while a superposition state shows observable probability oscillations.

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t=Ĥψ; stationary state ψ∝e^(−iEt/ℏ)

From postulates to results

  1. Turn the plane-wave energy–momentum relations E=ℏω and p=ℏk into operator actions to obtain the Schrödinger equation.
  2. Separating variables gives the time phase factor.
  3. Superposing two stationary states gives an oscillation frequency ΔE/h.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Worked example and check

For a superposition of two levels separated by ΔE=1 eV, the occupation probabilities oscillate at ν=ΔE/h≈2.42×10¹⁴ Hz, in the near-infrared band (visible light requires 1.8–3.1 eV, i.e. 4–7.9×10¹⁴ Hz); the quantitative mechanism of transitions and light emission is established by time-dependent perturbation theory.

Range of applicability

Nonrelativistic with conserved particle number; high speeds or creation–annihilation processes require quantum field theory; evolution restarts after a measurement.

Advanced topic · About 30–60 min

The Ehrenfest theorem and wave-packet motion

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Schrödinger lesson; the concept of expectation values. If the prerequisites are shaky, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The evolution of expectation values follows equations of classical form: d⟨x⟩/dt=⟨p⟩/m and d⟨p⟩/dt=−⟨∇V⟩ — the center of the wave packet moves along a Newtonian trajectory, with quantum effects hidden in the fluctuations and the nonlinearity of the potential; the spreading of the packet comes from the phase evolution of the momentum distribution.

d⟨A⟩/dt=(i/ℏ)⟨[H,A]⟩; free Gaussian σₓ(t)=σₓ₀√(1+(ℏt/2mσₓ₀²)²)

From postulates to results

  1. Take the time derivative of an expectation value and use the Schrödinger equation and Hermiticity to get the general relation.
  2. A=x gives the velocity relation; A=p with [p,V]=−iℏ∇V gives the force relation.
  3. For a quadratic potential the equations close and reproduce the classical orbit exactly.
  4. For a general potential ⟨∇V⟩≠∇V(⟨x⟩); the difference is the quantum correction.
  5. A free Gaussian wave packet spreads according to the formula above.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Worked example and check

For an electron wave packet with initial σₓ₀=1 μm, the characteristic spreading time is 2mσₓ₀²/ℏ≈1.7×10⁻⁸ s — significant spreading within nanoseconds; for macroscopic masses it is too slow to notice, and this is why the classical particle picture holds.

Range of applicability

The expectation-value equations generally do not close; when the fluctuations are large, the classical-trajectory description fails.

Phase differences among the momentum components make the packet disperse and spread; the larger the mass, the slower.

A first look at path integrals

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Schrödinger equation; the calculus of variations and action. If the prerequisites are shaky, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Feynman wrote the quantum amplitude as a sum over all paths: each path contributes e^(iS/ℏ), and the classical path wins out because its phase is stationary. It is equivalent to the Schrödinger equation, yet it is the backbone language of field theory and statistical mechanics.

K(b,a)=∫D[x(t)] e^(iS[x]/ℏ)

From postulates to results

  1. Slice time into small intervals and insert a completeness relation in each segment; the propagator becomes a multiple path integral.
  2. Neighboring paths differ in phase by ~S/ℏ, and non-classical paths cancel.
  3. As ℏ→0, stationary phase gives the principle of stationary action.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Worked example and check

Double-slit interference is the minimal version of the path integral: the phase difference of two paths determines bright and dark; the propagators of the free particle and the harmonic oscillator can be computed exactly — the oscillator's propagator (the Mehler kernel) is √(mω/(2πiℏ·sinωT))·e^(iS_cl/ℏ), with the extra factor being the normalization prefactor; the Hermite polynomials belong to the energy eigenfunctions and do not appear in this closed form of the propagator.

Range of applicability

For general potentials the measure and convergence of the path integral need careful handling; real-time oscillatory integrals are often computed after rotating to imaginary time.

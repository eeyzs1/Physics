Start with probability amplitudes

Lesson goal: explain "start with probability amplitudes" in your own words and independently complete the calculations and conditions-of-validity judgments below.

Prerequisites: waves; probability; the advanced lessons require complex numbers, linear algebra, and calculus. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

Think about a question first

Two outcomes have probabilities 0.3 and P. What is P? Write down your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understand the model

The quantum state gives the probability rule for measurement outcomes. The wave function is generally complex; its squared absolute value is a position probability density, not the probability at a single point; you must integrate over a spatial interval to get a probability. For discrete outcomes, the probability is obtained from the squared modulus of the amplitude.

Pᵢ = ∣aᵢ∣²; ΣPᵢ = 1

Order of use: identify the object of study and the conditions → make the units consistent → write down the equations → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The precise meaning and conventions of the variables are given in the worked examples below.

Work through the examples with the reasoning

A two-level state has two amplitudes √3/2 and 1/2, which give measurement probabilities 3/4 and 1/4 respectively. Normalization of probabilities does not mean that the outcome of a single measurement can be determined in advance.

Check the boundary cases

A superposition of quantum states must not be read as a classical object that has secretly settled on one outcome already.

Look back at the example: name at least one factor that was neglected, and explain under what circumstances it could affect the result. After changing one condition, does the original formula still apply?

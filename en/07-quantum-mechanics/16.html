Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Barrier tunneling and finite potential wells

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Schrödinger equation; the one-dimensional potential wells lesson. If the prerequisites are shaky, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

A particle with energy below the barrier still has a nonzero probability of getting through: the wave function decays exponentially in the classically forbidden region, and the remaining amplitude becomes a traveling wave again on the far side. The transmission is exponentially sensitive to the barrier width — the common basis of α decay, the scanning tunneling microscope, and flash memory.

T≈16E(V₀−E)/V₀²·e^(−2κa), κ=√(2m(V₀−E))/ℏ

From postulates to results

  1. Write the Schrödinger equation region by region: e^(±κx) inside the barrier, oscillatory solutions on both sides.
  2. Match the wave function and its derivative at the interfaces.
  3. In the thick-barrier limit the dominant term of the transmission is e^(−2κa).
  4. The finite well works the same way; the exponential tail outside the well pushes the levels slightly below those of the infinite well, and the thinner the barrier, the larger the downward shift.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Worked example and check

An electron meeting a square barrier with V₀−E=1 eV and a=1 nm: 2κa≈10.2, T≈e^(−10.2)≈4×10⁻⁵; doubling the thickness gives T≈10⁻⁹ — this exponential sensitivity is what lets an STM resolve surface corrugations at the 0.01 nm level.

Range of applicability

Single-particle stationary-state tunneling; many-body systems, strong correlations, and ultrafast time resolution need dedicated theories; for general potentials use the WKB semiclassical approximation.

Independent calculation

Show solution

The decay exponent is linear in the barrier width a; doubling the width doubles the exponent.

Transfer and counterexample

Why do α-decay half-lives span more than 20 orders of magnitude while α energies differ by only about a factor of two?

Check your solution to the transfer problem

The tunneling rate is exponentially sensitive to the barrier width and the energy difference; a small change in energy is amplified by e^(−2κa) into astronomically different half-lives (the Geiger–Nuttall law).

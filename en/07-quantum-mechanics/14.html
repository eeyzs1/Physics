Perturbation theory: how to estimate energy-level corrections

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: eigenstates; inner products; Hamiltonians. If the prerequisites are shaky, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

When the full problem cannot be solved exactly but deviates only slightly from a solvable model, expand in powers of the perturbation strength. The nondegenerate first-order energy correction is the expectation value of the perturbation in the unperturbed state.

H=H₀+λV; Eₙ≈Eₙ⁽⁰⁾+λ〈n|V|n〉

From postulates to results

  1. Expand the energies and eigenstates in powers of λ and substitute into the Schrödinger equation.
  2. Collect the first-order terms.
  3. Left-multiply by 〈n| and use the eigenvalue relations of H₀ to obtain the first-order energy correction.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Worked example and check

A real-system example: the n=2 level of hydrogen is fourfold degenerate; in a weak electric field you must first diagonalize the perturbation within the degenerate subspace (degenerate perturbation theory), which gives the linear Stark effect ΔE=3ea₀E; the ground state is nondegenerate and shows only a second-order (quadratic) Stark effect. An abstract example: with unperturbed energy 5 energy units, λ=0.1, and 〈V〉=2, the first-order approximation is 5.2. Off-diagonal terms also affect the state corrections and higher-order energies.

Range of applicability

Nondegenerate levels and a sufficiently small perturbation; near degeneracy, diagonalize within the degenerate subspace first.

Independent calculation

Show solution

3+0.2×(−2)=2.6.

Transfer and counterexample

If the first-order energy correction of a state vanishes, can you conclude the perturbation has no effect at all?

Check your solution to the transfer problem

No; the second-order energy and the first-order state correction can both be nonzero — you must also check the selection rules and the off-diagonal matrix elements.

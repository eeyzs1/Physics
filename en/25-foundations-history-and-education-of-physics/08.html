The scientific method: induction, falsification, and paradigms

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the lesson on models and evidence; history of science. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

The growth mechanism of scientific knowledge has three pieces: induction raises observations to laws, deduction derives testable predictions from principles, and the falsifiability principle (Popper) demands that theories take risks; paradigms (Kuhn) explain the structure of normal science and of revolutions — a reflective framework for physics education.

testability = falsifiability; paradigm = shared exemplars for problem-solving

From assumptions to results

  1. Induction has no logical necessity (Hume's problem); science reinforces it with reproducible experiments.
  2. Deduction guarantees internal consistency, but its content comes from the assumptions.
  3. Popper: a theory must forbid some observations to count as "scientific"; unfalsifiable statements add no knowledge.
  4. Kuhn: normal science solves puzzles within a paradigm, and accumulating anomalies trigger revolutions (relativity replacing the ether is the classic example).
  5. Lakatos reconciled the two with research programmes.

Worked example and check

General relativity's advance predictions of light deflection and gravitational redshift (risky, and they passed) are the textbook case of the falsificationist standard; cold fusion was refuted because it could not be reproduced; physics education research (PER) itself uses this same apparatus to test teaching methods (linking to the lesson on teaching methods).

Conditions of validity

Scientific practice is more complex than the standard (auxiliary hypotheses can "protect" a theory); the methodology itself is also evolving.

Independent computation

Show solution

A theory must rule out some possible observations, so that experience can in principle refute it.

Transfer and counterexamples

Why would an "untestable theory of everything" be criticized as unscientific?

Show solution to the transfer exercise

If it forbids no observations, it cannot be falsified and, by Popper's standard, produces no empirical knowledge — the core of the string-theory controversy.

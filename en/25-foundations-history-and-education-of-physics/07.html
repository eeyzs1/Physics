Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Experiments that changed physics: oil drops, scattering, and quantum transitions

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the lesson on experimental method; the concepts of charge and the atom. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

Three classic experiments shaped the foundations of modern physics: the Millikan oil-drop experiment demonstrated the quantization of charge, Rutherford scattering discovered the atomic nucleus, and Franck–Hertz demonstrated that atomic energy levels are discrete — together they showed that "ingenious apparatus + quantitative measurement > authoritative intuition".

mg=6πηrv (with Stokes-drag correction); scattering count rate ∝1/sin⁴(θ/2)

From assumptions to results

  1. The oil drop is balanced under gravity, air buoyancy, and Stokes drag; after X-rays change its charge, the difference in balancing voltage for the same drop yields the elementary charge.
  2. The large-angle scattering rate of α particles far exceeded the Thomson model's prediction; only a nuclear model with the positive charge concentrated at the center could explain the 1/sin⁴ angular distribution.
  3. Collisions of electrons with mercury vapor are elastic below 4.9 eV and excite spectral lines above it, proving that the energy levels are discrete.

Worked example and check

Millikan obtained e≈1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C; in 1911 Rutherford deduced from gold-foil scattering a nuclear radius of ~10⁻¹⁴ m, ten-thousand times smaller than the atom; the 4.9 eV peak of Franck–Hertz (1914; Nobel Prize 1925) corresponds directly to the 253.6 nm mercury line.

Conditions of validity

A simplified pedagogical account; the systematic-error corrections in the original experiments (Brownian motion and evaporation of the oil drops; spin corrections to scattering) are themselves lessons in methodology.

Transfer and counterexamples

Why could large-angle scattering conclusively rule out the uniform atom model?

Show solution to the transfer exercise

The Coulomb deflection of α particles by a uniform sphere of positive charge is too small, and even the superposition of multiple Coulomb scatterings cannot produce the observed 1/sin⁴ distribution — a concentrated nucleus is the only explanation.

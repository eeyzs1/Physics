Computing a Two-Level System from the Partition Function

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: probability; exponential functions; energy levels. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The canonical ensemble describes a system in contact with a heat bath at constant temperature. The thermal-equilibrium probabilities are set jointly by energy and temperature; the partition function guarantees normalization and organizes the microscopic energy levels into computable macroscopic quantities.

Z=Σe^(−βEᵢ); pᵢ=e^(−βEᵢ)/Z; ⟨E⟩=−∂lnZ/∂β; F=−kBT·lnZ

From assumptions to results

  1. Take the energy levels to be 0 and ε, both non-degenerate.
  2. Z=1+e^(−βε).
  3. The excitation probability is 1/(1+e^(βε)).
  4. The mean energy is ε·p_excited.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point out the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Stuck? Show the key intermediate steps
  1. ⟨E⟩=ΣEᵢe^(−βEᵢ)/Z: the numerator is exactly −∂Z/∂β, hence ⟨E⟩=−∂lnZ/∂β.
  2. Similarly, F=−k_BT·lnZ connects the partition function to a thermodynamic potential.
  3. The entropy is S=−∂F/∂T= k_B(lnZ+β⟨E⟩).
  4. Substituting Z=1+e^(−βε) reproduces the mean energy and excitation probability computed term by term earlier, and the heat capacity shows a Schottky peak at βε≈2.4.

If you are still stuck, write down the specific equation before discussing it — that is more effective than rereading repeatedly.

Example and check

If ε/(kBT)=ln3, the excitation probability is 1/4; as the temperature tends to infinity the two states become equally probable — not all particles are excited.

Range of applicability

Assumes weak coupling to the heat bath and thermal equilibrium; when the levels are degenerate, degeneracy factors must be included.

Independent calculation

Show solution

p_excited=1/(1+4)=0.2.

Transfer and counterexample

If the excited state is twofold degenerate and the ground state is non-degenerate, what does the previous probability become?

Check the transfer-problem solution

The total excited-state weight is 2/4=1/2 and the ground-state weight is 1, so the excitation probability is (1/2)/(3/2)=1/3.

Advanced self-test

One step more than the previous problem, or a unit conversion is needed; on review days 1, 3, and 7, switch to this problem to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

The ratio is e^(−βε)=e^(−2)≈0.135.

  3. Within two days, redo both self-tests without looking at the solutions; for each step you get wrong, return to the corresponding section.

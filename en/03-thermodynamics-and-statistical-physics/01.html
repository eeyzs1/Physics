How Energy Enters and Leaves a System

Lesson goal: be able to explain "how energy enters and leaves a system" in your own words, and independently complete the calculations and conditions-of-validity judgments below.

Prerequisites: classical mechanics; probability, with calculus needed for the advanced parts. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

Start with a question

A system absorbs 80 J of heat and does 20 J of work on its surroundings; by how many joules does its internal energy increase? Note your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understand the model

First delimit the system with a boundary, then record the energy exchanged through heat transfer and work. Here we adopt the convention that Q is the heat absorbed by the system and W is the work done by the system on its surroundings. Heat is energy in transit; temperature is a property of state.

ΔU = Q − W

Order of use: identify the object of study and the conditions → make the units consistent → write the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The precise meanings and conventions of the variables are given in the worked example below.

Work through the example with reasoning

A gas absorbs 100 J of heat while pushing a piston to do 30 J of work on the surroundings, so its internal energy increases by 70 J. Adiabatic only means Q = 0; it does not mean the temperature is necessarily unchanged.

Check the boundary cases

Heat and temperature are not synonyms; the sign convention for work done on the surroundings must be stated in advance.

Look back at the example: name at least one neglected factor and explain in what circumstances it could affect the result. After one condition is changed, does the original formula still apply?

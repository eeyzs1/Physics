Phase Transitions and Critical Exponents: The Ising Model

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: free energy and phase transitions; probability. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The Ising model captures the universal structure of continuous phase transitions with spins and nearest-neighbor interactions: above the critical point there is no spontaneous magnetization, below it the symmetry is spontaneously broken; the critical exponents depend only on dimension and symmetry (the universality class).

H=−J Σ⟨ij⟩ sᵢsⱼ; mean-field Tc=zJ/kB

From assumptions to results

  1. Replacing the nearest-neighbor interactions by an effective mean field gives the self-consistency equation m=tanh(βzJm).
  2. Expanding near Tc gives m∝(Tc−T)^(1/2).
  3. Comparison with Onsager's exact solution shows that mean field overestimates Tc and gets the exponents wrong.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point out the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Example and check

For the two-dimensional square lattice with z=4: mean field gives Tc=4J/kB, while Onsager's exact solution gives Tc=2J/ln(1+√2)≈2.27J/kB — mean field overestimates by about 76%; the exact critical exponent is β=1/8 rather than 1/2.

Range of applicability

Mean field neglects fluctuations; the critical region requires the renormalization group, and outside a universality class there is no universality.

Independent calculation

Mean-field β=1/2: m∝(Tc−T)^0.5.

Transfer and counterexample

Why are the critical exponents more "universal" than Tc itself?

Check the transfer-problem solution

The exponents are determined only by the spatial dimension and the symmetry of the order parameter; details of the microscopic couplings do not change them.

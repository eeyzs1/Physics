The Third Law, Absolute Entropy, and Negative Temperature

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the entropy lesson; the statistical-mechanics skeleton lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Nernst's theorem: the entropy of a perfect crystal tends to a constant (which may be taken as zero) as T→0 — the third law of thermodynamics, from which absolute entropy follows; for a system with a bounded energy spectrum, on the high-energy side the entropy decreases with energy, defining a negative temperature that is "hotter" than any positive temperature.

S(T→0)→S₀; negative-temperature condition ∂S/∂E<0

From assumptions to results

  1. In ordinary systems S(E) is increasing and concave, so 1/T=∂S/∂E>0.
  2. Spin systems have a bounded spectrum; S(E) first rises and then falls, and the high-energy side gives negative T.
  3. When a negative-temperature system contacts a positive-temperature system, energy flows toward the positive-temperature side — negative temperature is hotter.
  4. Another statement of the third law: absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps.

Example and check

For an S=1/2 spin system, β→−∞ corresponds to a fully population-inverted, high-energy ordered state; in 1951 Purcell and Pound realized negative temperature in the nuclear spins of lithium fluoride, and in 2013 it was reproduced in a cold atomic gas.

Range of applicability

Negative temperature requires a bounded spectrum and a quasi-isolated system; translational degrees of freedom are unbounded, so the system as a whole cannot be at negative temperature.

Independent calculation

Show solution

A negative temperature β<0 is hotter than any positive temperature, so energy flows into the positive-temperature system.

The third law: as T→0 the entropy change tends to zero, and the number of isothermal–adiabatic stages needed to reach 0 K diverges.

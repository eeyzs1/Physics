From Particles to Pressure

Lesson goal: be able to explain "from particles to pressure" in your own words, and independently complete the calculations and conditions-of-validity judgments below.

Prerequisites: classical mechanics; probability, with calculus needed for the advanced parts. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

Start with a question

An ideal gas held at constant volume is heated from 200 K to 600 K; by what factor does its pressure change? Note your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understand the model

Gas molecules striking the walls of the container produce the macroscopic pressure. The ideal gas neglects the effects of molecular volume and interaction potential energy and applies to sufficiently dilute gases; temperature must be expressed in kelvin.

pV = nRT

Order of use: identify the object of study and the conditions → make the units consistent → write the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The precise meanings and conventions of the variables are given in the worked example below.

Work through the example with reasoning

With the amount of substance and the volume held fixed, raising the temperature from 300 K to 600 K doubles the pressure. If the temperature rises from 20 °C to 40 °C, the absolute temperature has not doubled, and the pressure will not double either.

Check the boundary cases

An ideal gas deviates from pV=nRT at high pressure and low temperature; comparing pressures requires absolute temperature (kelvin), and going from 20 °C to 40 °C is not a doubling of temperature.

Look back at the example: name at least one neglected factor and explain in what circumstances it could affect the result. After one condition is changed, does the original formula still apply?

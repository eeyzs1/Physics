Free Energy and the Simplest Model of Phase Transitions

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: calculus; equilibrium and stability. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

At fixed temperature and volume, the equilibrium state makes the Helmholtz free energy take its appropriate minimum. An order-parameter expansion writes symmetry and stability in explicitly: an even function reflects the equivalence of the two directions, and a positive quartic term limits large amplitudes.

F(m)=am²/2+bm⁴/4; b>0

From assumptions to results

  1. Set dF/dm=am+bm³=0.
  2. Candidate solutions: m=0 or m²=−a/b.
  3. Then use the second derivative to decide which are minima. For a>0 the zero solution is stable; for a<0 two nonzero stable branches appear.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point out the equation where you got stuck, then ask me about it.

Example and check

With a=−4 and b=1, the nonzero minima lie at m=±2; the second derivative at the origin is negative, so it is not a stable equilibrium.

Range of applicability

This is a mean-field expansion; critical fluctuations, spatial inhomogeneity, and first-order phase transitions may lie beyond its scope.

Independent calculation

Show solution

m=√(−a/b)=3; the second derivative a+3bm²=18>0.

Transfer and counterexample

Why can't the quartic term be dropped while still discussing a<0?

Check the transfer-problem solution

With only a negative quadratic term, the free energy would decrease without bound as |m| grows, and the model would have no stable minimum.

Advanced self-test

One step more than the previous problem, or a unit conversion is needed; on review days 1, 3, and 7, switch to this problem to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

m=√(16/4)=2.

Before moving to the next stage

  1. Substitute this lesson's formulas into a numerical case of your own invention, estimating the order of magnitude first and computing precisely afterwards.
  2. Explain how the conclusion would change if the strongest assumption in the derivation were removed.
  3. At the next review, do the advanced problem before the basic one, and compare the time spent and error causes on the two attempts.

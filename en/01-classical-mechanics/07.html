Calculus of Variations and the Euler–Lagrange Equation

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case on your own, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the pendulum's derivation from the action; integration. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

The laws of nature often make the action stationary. Once generalized coordinates are chosen, one Euler–Lagrange equation yields the equation of motion for each coordinate at once — equivalent to Newton's approach, but more systematic.

d/dt(∂L/∂q̇)−∂L/∂q=0

From assumptions to results

  1. Integrate δS=δ∫L dt by parts; the fixed endpoints make the boundary term vanish.
  2. Since δq is arbitrary, the Euler–Lagrange expression of the integrand vanishes point by point.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Substituting L=½mẋ²−½kx²: ∂L/∂ẋ=mẋ, whose time derivative is mẍ, and ∂L/∂x=−kx, giving mẍ=−kx, in agreement with Newton's result. With m=2 kg and k=8 N/m, ω=2 rad/s.

Range of applicability

A stationary value need not be a minimum; nonholonomic constraints and non-conservative forces require extensions using Lagrange multipliers or a dissipation function.

Independent calculation

Show solution

ω=√(k/m)=2; the E-L equation gives mẍ=−kx directly.

Transfer and counterexample

What conclusion follows when L does not contain a generalized coordinate q?

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

∂L/∂q̇ is constant, i.e. the generalized momentum conjugate to a cyclic coordinate is conserved.

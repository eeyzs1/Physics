Inertia Tensor and Rotation in Three Dimensions: Principal Axes, Euler's Equations, and Precession

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case on your own, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the lesson on rigid-body rotation; vectors and matrices. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

For a three-dimensional rigid body the moment of inertia is a tensor, not a single number: axes in different directions give different moments of inertia, and only for rotation about a principal axis is the angular momentum parallel to the angular velocity. Earth's polar motion and the precession of a top are both governed by this tensor structure.

L_z=I_zzω_z (principal axis); Euler's equations I₁ω̇₁=(I₂−I₃)ω₂ω₃ (cyclic)

From assumptions to results

  1. For each particle write mr²−mr_ir_j and sum over particles to obtain the nine components of the inertia tensor.
  2. Both the rotational kinetic energy and the angular momentum are written as contractions with it.
  3. A real symmetric tensor can be diagonalized; in the principal-axis frame the angular momentum is parallel to the angular velocity.
  4. With no torque acting, Euler's equations show that rotation about the intermediate principal axis is unstable (the tennis racket theorem).

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

For a uniform sphere about any diameter, I=2mR²/5 — the same about the polar and equatorial axes, so there is no precession; Earth is slightly flattened, its equatorial moment of inertia exceeds the polar one by about 0.3%, producing the Chandler wobble on the order of 430 days; a spinning top under a gravitational torque precesses slowly about the vertical axis, with Ω≈mgr/(I₃ω₃).

Range of applicability

Rigid body and principal-axis coordinate frame; Earth's liquid core and deformational damping make the real polar motion deviate from the ideal model.

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

Linearizing Euler's equations shows that perturbations near the intermediate axis grow exponentially — the axes of largest and smallest moment are stable, the intermediate one is not.

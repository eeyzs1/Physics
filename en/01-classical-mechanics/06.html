Central Forces and the Kepler Problem

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case on your own, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: angular momentum; universal gravitation. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

A central force exerts zero torque about the center, so angular momentum is conserved and the motion is confined to a plane. An inverse-square gravitational force gives conic-section orbits and Kepler's three laws.

L=mr²ω conserved; circular orbit T²=4π²r³/(GM)

From assumptions to results

  1. In polar coordinates the radial equation is m(r̈−rω²)=F(r).
  2. For a circular orbit set rω²=GM/r².
  3. Substituting ω=2π/T yields Kepler's third law.
  4. Conservation of angular momentum gives the increased speed at perihelion.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Take GM=4×10¹⁴ m³/s² (roughly Earth) and a circular orbit with r=10⁷ m: T²=4π²×10²¹/(4×10¹⁴)≈9.87×10⁷ s², so T≈9.9×10³ s≈2.8 h, consistent with the order of magnitude of a near-Earth orbit.

Range of applicability

Two bodies, no perturbations, Newtonian gravity; strong fields or high speeds require general relativity, and many bodies require numerical methods.

Transfer and counterexample

A satellite in an elliptical orbit has a higher speed at perihelion; which conserved quantity accounts for this?

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

Angular momentum mr v⊥ is conserved, and together with energy conservation it determines the orbit's shape.

Before moving on to the next stage

  1. Plug this lesson's formulas into a numerical case of your own invention, estimating the order of magnitude first and then computing precisely.
  2. Explain how the conclusion would change if you removed the strongest assumption in the derivation.
  3. At your next review, do the advanced problem before the basic one, and compare the time spent and the causes of your errors across the two attempts.

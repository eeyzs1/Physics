Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case on your own, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the lesson on Hamiltonians; the concept of action. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

Phase-space coordinates are not unique: a canonical transformation preserves the Poisson bracket structure and turns a complicated Hamiltonian into an integrable form; generating functions (F₁–F₄) and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation turn "solving the equations" into "finding good coordinates" — an advanced key to classical mechanics.

{Q,P}=1 preserved; dF/dt gives the transformation

From assumptions to results

  1. The canonical condition is equivalent to preservation of the symplectic structure.
  2. The four types of generating functions generate the map (q,p)↔(Q,P) from different pairs of variables.
  3. If the transformation makes the new Hamiltonian K depend only on the momenta, the Qᵢ are cyclic and the motion is solved at once.
  4. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation looks for the principal function S that makes K=0; its solution is precisely the action function — isomorphic to the WKB/optical eikonal (the link to the quantum mechanics lessons).

Example and check

The action–angle variables generated for the harmonic oscillator by F₂=(mω/2)q²cotQ give H=ωP: P=E/ω is conserved and Q advances uniformly, making the orbit geometry plain at a glance; canonical perturbation theory (celestial-mechanics perturbations) is developed on this basis.

Range of applicability

Global topology (generating functions become multivalued at caustics) and constrained systems require extensions; quantization corresponds to unitary transformations.

Deriving the Pendulum Equation from the Action

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case on your own, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: calculus; kinetic and potential energy. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

Generalized coordinates encode geometric constraints directly. The pendulum's position is described by the angle θ; the string length is fixed, so the tension need not be solved for as an unknown. The stationarity condition on the action yields the equation of motion.

L=T−V; d/dt(∂L/∂θ̇)−∂L/∂θ=0

From assumptions to results

  1. Take T=mℓ²θ̇²/2 and V=mgℓ(1−cosθ).
  2. Differentiating both terms gives mℓ²θ̈+mgℓsinθ=0.
  3. At small angles, further apply sinθ≈θ.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

With ℓ=1 m and g=9.8 m/s², the small-amplitude angular frequency is about 3.13 rad/s. The mass cancels out, so in the ideal model the small-amplitude period does not depend on the mass.

Range of applicability

Fixed string length, the bob treated as a point particle, and no drag; at large angles you must keep sinθ and can no longer use the isochronous approximation.

Transfer and counterexample

Why can't you describe the entire swing by simply replacing θ̈ with a constant?

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

The restoring torque varies with the angle, and the angular acceleration varies with position; a constant-acceleration approximation is valid only over very short intervals.

Small Oscillations and Normal Coordinates: The Common Language of Coupled Systems

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case on your own, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: Lagrangian mechanics; matrix eigenvalues. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

Small displacements from equilibrium can always be linearized into M·q̈=−K·q; solving the eigenvalue problem decomposes the coupled oscillations into independent normal modes. Molecular vibrational spectra, structural vibrations, and phonons all start from here.

det(K−ω²M)=0; each mode oscillates independently

From assumptions to results

  1. Expand the potential energy about the equilibrium point to quadratic terms and the kinetic energy to quadratic terms in the velocities to obtain M and K.
  2. Set q=a·e^(−iωt) to obtain the generalized eigenvalue problem (K−ω²M)a=0.
  3. The eigenvectors define the normal coordinates, in which the Lagrangian decouples into a sum of independent oscillators.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Two equal masses m coupled by three springs k, k, κ (ends fixed): the in-phase mode has ω²=k/m (the middle spring is not stretched), and the out-of-phase mode has ω²=(k+2κ)/m; for m=1, k=4, κ=2 these are 4 and 8 s⁻² respectively — the same mathematical structure as the two-pendulum model in the lesson on normal modes.

Range of applicability

Linear approximation: at large amplitudes anharmonic terms couple the modes; for degenerate modes the eigenvectors are not unique.

Independent calculation

Show solution

ω²=(k+2κ)/m=(4+4)/1=8.

Transfer and counterexample

Why can any initial state be written as a superposition of normal modes?

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

The eigenvectors form a complete basis (M and K are symmetric and positive definite), and the general solution of the linear equations is a linear combination of the modes.

Advanced self-test

One more step than the previous problem, or a change of units; when you review on days 1, 3, and 7, switch to this problem to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

ω²=(k+2κ)/m=(8+8)/2=8.

CT and the Radon transform

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: Fourier concepts; the X-ray attenuation lesson. If the prerequisites are shaky, first return to mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Computed tomography measures projections from hundreds of angles and then inverts the Radon transform to reconstruct the slice: the central-slice theorem connects the projections to two-dimensional Fourier space, filtered back-projection is the engineering implementation, and the HU calibration turns attenuation into comparable tissue values.

Radon: Fourier slice theorem for projections; HU=1000×(μ−μ_water)/μ_water

From assumptions to results

  1. The projection at each angle corresponds to a line through the center of two-dimensional Fourier space.
  2. Filling in many angles covers the frequency domain; the inverse transform gives the tomographic image.
  3. Direct back-projection produces star artifacts; the projections must first be ramp-filtered.
  4. HU assigns water 0, air −1000, and bone about +1000; window width and level adjust the display.

Example and check

A chest CT takes a few seconds at a dose of about 5–7 mSv (linking to the dosimetry lesson); abdominal CT can resolve 1 mm lesions; spiral CT plus ECG gating has brought cardiac imaging into routine use.

Boundaries of applicability

The line-integral assumption neglects scattering and beam hardening; iterative reconstruction lowers the dose.

Transfer and counterexample

Why does direct back-projection produce star artifacts?

Solution to the transfer problem

Simply summing the projections from all angles undersamples the high frequencies, leaving a bright center and blurry edges — it is unbiased only after ramp filtering restores the weights.

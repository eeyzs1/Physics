Imaging measures signals

Lesson goal: explain in your own words what "imaging measures signals" means, and independently complete the calculations and conditions-of-validity judgments below.

Prerequisites: thermal physics; electromagnetism; probability. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

First, think about a question

With initial intensity 100, what is the intensity after passing through two layers, each of which transmits half? Note your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understanding the model

Different medical imaging techniques measure different physical signals. X-ray imaging exploits differences in transmitted intensity (the difference in μ between soft tissue and bone is a few percent per millimeter; CT calibrates μ against water into HU values), MRI uses the nuclear magnetic resonance signal (the principles are covered in the specialized lessons of the biophysics field), and ultrasound uses the reflection of sound waves. A simplified single-energy, narrow-beam attenuation model relates material thickness to transmittance.

I = I₀e^(−μx)

Order of use: identify the system and its conditions → make the units consistent → write the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The specific meanings and conventions of the variables are given in the example below.

Worked example with reasoning

If a layer of a given thickness has transmittance 1/2, then after an identical second layer the transmittance becomes 1/4. Real imaging also involves scattering, the energy spectrum, and reconstruction algorithms; image brightness is not a direct photograph of the substance.

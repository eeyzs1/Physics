Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Optical tweezers and single-molecule mechanics

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: momentum and force; the Brownian-motion lesson. If the prerequisites are shaky, first return to mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The gradient force of a focused laser beam can trap micron-scale beads and exert piconewton-level forces — optical tweezers turn single-molecule mechanics into measurable experiments: stepping motors, DNA stretching, and folding transitions have all been quantified this way.

Gradient-force trapping; stiffness κ~0.1 pN/nm

From assumptions to results

  1. A bead with a high refractive index is pushed toward the intensity maximum, while the scattering force acts along the radiation-pressure direction; the balance of the two forms a three-dimensional trap.
  2. The trap is approximately harmonic; the displacement-to-force calibration is obtained from the Brownian-motion spectrum (equipartition).
  3. Piconewton and nanometer resolution is just right for the chemical steps of molecular motors (linking back to the thermal physics lesson, k_BT=4.1 pN·nm).

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Myosin steps along actin in 5 nm increments with forces of about 3–5 pN (about 10 k_BT per step); DNA's 65 pN overstretching transition and its roughly 15 pN unzipping force were measured with optical tweezers (benchmark data from 2001 reviews of single-molecule biophysics).

Boundaries of applicability

Near-field effects and thermal noise set the limits; high precision requires feedback and a cooled chamber.

Transfer and counterexample

Why are optical tweezers well suited to measuring motor proteins?

Solution to the transfer problem

Piconewton forces and nanometer steps fall exactly within the tweezers' resolution and the molecular motors' operating range, with no disturbance from mechanical contact.

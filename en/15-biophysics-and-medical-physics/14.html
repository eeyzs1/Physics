Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

The physics of magnetic resonance imaging: spins, relaxation, and gradient encoding

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Zeeman-effect lesson; Fourier concepts. If the prerequisites are shaky, first return to mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

MRI uses radiofrequency pulses to tip the magnetization of water protons in tissue and then forms images from the relaxing signal. Gradient magnetic fields make protons at different positions resonate at different frequencies, turning spatial encoding into frequency–phase encoding; k-space data are reconstructed into an image by a Fourier transform.

ω=γB (for protons γ/2π≈42.58 MHz/T); M_z=M₀(1−e^(−t/T₁))

From assumptions to results

  1. Spins precess at the Larmor frequency in the static field B₀.
  2. A 90° radiofrequency pulse tips the magnetization into the transverse plane; it then relaxes with T₁ (longitudinal recovery) and T₂ (transverse dephasing).
  3. Superposing a linear gradient B=B₀+G·r makes the resonance frequency carry position information; k-space is filled step by step and the image reconstructed by an inverse Fourier transform.
  4. The choice of TR and TE determines T₁ or T₂ weighting.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

At the clinical field strength of 1.5 T, protons resonate at 63.9 MHz (127.7 MHz at 3 T); short TR/short TE gives T₁ weighting (fat bright), long TR/long TE gives T₂ weighting (fluid bright) — tissue contrast comes from differences in the relaxation parameters.

Boundaries of applicability

Classical magnetization-vector picture; BOLD functional imaging and diffusion tensor imaging need further models; ferromagnetic implants face safety limits in gradient fields.

Transfer and counterexample

Why is MRI's soft-tissue contrast better than that of X-ray CT?

Solution to the transfer problem

The MRI signal depends on hydrogen density and relaxation parameters such as T₁/T₂, which differ markedly between soft tissues; X-ray attenuation coefficients are nearly identical across soft tissues and often require contrast agents.

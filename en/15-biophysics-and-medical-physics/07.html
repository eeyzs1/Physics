Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Radiation dose and protection: Gy, Sv, and ALARA

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: energy and ionization concepts; exponential decay. If the prerequisites are shaky, first return to mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Medicine and nuclear technology turn "how much radiation" into measurable, manageable quantities: absorbed dose in grays (J/kg), equivalent/effective dose in sieverts weighted by biological effectiveness, and activity in becquerels; protection follows ALARA — as low as reasonably achievable.

1 Gy=1 J/kg; H=Σw_R·D; 1 Sv=1 J/kg (weighted)

From assumptions to results

  1. Absorbed dose measures the energy deposited per unit mass.
  2. The biological effectiveness of different radiations differs by a factor of 1–20; radiation weighting factors w_R correct for this to give the equivalent dose, which is then summed with tissue weighting factors to give the effective dose for comparing different exposure geometries.
  3. Activity (Bq) describes the number of decays per second of a source; it connects to dose through exposure geometry and shielding.

Example and check

A chest CT typically delivers an effective dose of about 5–7 mSv, background radiation is about 2–3 mSv per year, and a single chest X-ray is about 0.02 mSv; aircrew receive an additional 2–5 mSv per year — protection design compares these quantities with the limits (20 mSv/year occupational) and optimizes according to ALARA.

Boundaries of applicability

Dosimetry models and epidemiological extrapolations across populations; individual risks and benefits must be assessed by professionals, and this lesson is not a substitute for medical advice.

Transfer and counterexample

Why is external α-particle exposure only mildly hazardous while internal exposure is severe?

Solution to the transfer problem

The range of an α particle is extremely short (stopped by paper), so external exposure cannot reach sensitive layers; once inhaled, it deposits locally in lung tissue, and the high ionization density produces a large equivalent dose.

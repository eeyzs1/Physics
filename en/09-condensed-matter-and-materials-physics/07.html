Magnetic Order: The Weiss Molecular Field, Curie–Weiss, and Hysteresis

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the concept of phase transitions; the statistical-ensemble lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

A ferromagnet magnetizes spontaneously below the Curie temperature: the Weiss molecular field averages the interactions, giving the Curie–Weiss law and spontaneous symmetry breaking; real hysteresis loops are determined by domains and pinning, and coercivity is a lever for materials engineering.

Curie–Weiss χ=C/(T−T_C); self-consistent m=tanh(m·T_C/T)

From assumptions to results

  1. The molecular-field assumption H_eff=H+λM brings the exchange interaction into a mean field.
  2. The self-consistent equation m=tanh(μB_eff/k_BT) has a nonzero solution for T<T_C; it saturates at low temperature and decays as √(T_C−T) in the critical region.
  3. The paramagnetic region gives χ=C/(T−T_C).
  4. Hysteresis arises from the irreversibility of domain-wall motion and moment reversal.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Iron has T_C=1043 K and a magnetic moment of about 2.2 μ_B per atom (non-integer because the electrons are itinerant); NdFeB has a coercivity of about 10⁶ A/m (hard magnetic) while silicon steel is about 1 A/m (soft magnetic) — the two extremes of the hysteresis loop.

Range of applicability

Mean field overestimates the critical exponent (the true β≈0.36); the microscopic mechanism requires Heisenberg or itinerant-electron models.

Independent calculation

Show solution

T_C≈1043 K.

Transfer and counterexample

Transformers use soft magnets and loudspeakers use hard magnets — why?

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

Soft magnets have low coercivity and small alternating-current losses; hard magnets have high remanence and coercivity and provide a constant magnetic field.

Superfluid Helium and the Two-Fluid Model

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Bose-statistics lesson; the concept of phonons. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

Liquid helium-4 enters the superfluid state at 2.17 K: it flows through capillaries with zero viscosity, creeps up walls as a film, and shows the fountain effect. The two-fluid model splits the liquid into a normal component (carrying entropy and viscosity) and a superfluid component (entropy-free and viscosity-free), explaining the coupled thermo-mechanical anomalies; the root cause is macroscopic occupation by bosons.

ρ=ρ_n+ρ_s; fountain pressure Δp=ρs·ΔT

From assumptions to results

  1. Write liquid helium as a superposition of two fictitious fluids.
  2. The fountain effect links heat and mechanics through entropy flow and chemical-potential equilibrium (Δp=ρsΔT).
  3. Second sound is the antiphase oscillation of the two components (a temperature wave).
  4. In rotation, quantized vortices replace rigid-body rotation of the whole fluid.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Near the λ point at 2.17 K the specific heat shows a λ-shaped peak; the superfluid film creeps up walls at about 30 cm/s; superfluid helium is a prototype system for quantum turbulence and for the interior physics of neutron stars.

Range of applicability

The two-fluid model is phenomenological; a quantitative description requires quantum field theory at finite temperature; the paired superfluidity of He-3 is more complex.

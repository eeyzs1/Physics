The Qualitative Picture of BCS Theory: Cooper Pairs and the Energy Gap

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the superconductivity lesson; the phonon lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

Through the phonon-mediated attraction, electrons pair into Cooper pairs (opposite momenta, spin singlet), and the condensation opens an energy gap 2Δ: a minimum energy is required to break a pair — zero resistance, the Meissner effect, and the exponential law in the specific heat all follow from the gap; the isotope effect confirms the phonon mechanism.

2Δ(0)≈3.52k_BT_c; Δ(T→T_c)∝√(T_c−T)

From assumptions to results

  1. Electrons near the Fermi surface acquire an effective attraction through virtual phonon exchange.
  2. Any attraction, however weak, destabilizes the Fermi sea (Cooper instability) to form bound pairs.
  3. The BCS variational construction gives the condensed ground state and the excitation spectrum E=√(ξ²+Δ²).
  4. T_c is determined by the Debye frequency and the electron–phonon coupling.
  5. The carriers are a macroscopic number of phase-coherent pairs.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

For aluminum 2Δ/k_BT_c≈3.3–3.5 (near the BCS value) and the coherence length is about 1.6 μm — far larger than the pair spacing, so millions of pairs overlap; the isotope effect in mercury, T_c∝M^(−1/2) (1950), pinned down the phonons; tunneling experiments measure Δ directly (Giaever, 1973 Nobel Prize).

Range of applicability

Weak-coupling BCS; the d-wave pairing of cuprate high-temperature superconductors lies beyond this picture.

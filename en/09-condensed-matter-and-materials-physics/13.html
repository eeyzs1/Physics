Phonons and the Specific Heat of Solids: From Einstein to Debye

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: simple harmonic motion; Bose statistics. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

The quantum of lattice vibration is the phonon. At high temperature each mode contributes k_BT (Dulong–Petit); at low temperature only long-wavelength acoustic modes remain excited, and the Debye model gives a specific heat ∝T³ — one of the earliest successes of quantum statistics.

Debye low-T: C≈(12π⁴/5)Nk_B(T/θ_D)³

From assumptions to results

  1. Quantize the 3N normal modes of N atoms; each mode has mean energy ℏω(n̄+½).
  2. The Einstein model takes a single frequency, recovering 3Nk_BT at high temperature and exponential freezing at low temperature.
  3. The Debye model integrates the density of states of the linear acoustic dispersion ω=v_s·k with a cutoff at 3N modes; at low temperature only modes with ω≲k_BT/ℏ participate, and the integral gives the T³ law.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

For copper θ_D≈343 K: at 300 K, C≈3R≈24.9 J/(mol·K); at 30 K the T³ law gives about 1.3 J/(mol·K), one order of magnitude smaller; the total low-temperature specific heat of a metal is C=γT+βT³, with the electron and phonon terms competing.

Range of applicability

Harmonic approximation; anharmonic effects (thermal expansion, thermal resistance) and optical branches require more complete models.

Transfer and counterexample

Why must the Einstein model fail at very low temperature?

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

It lacks the continuous spectrum of the acoustic branch with ω→0: long-wavelength modes should be excited at any low temperature, and the exponential freezing of a single frequency is too fast, inconsistent with the experimental T³ law.

The Free-Electron Gas and the pn Junction: From the Fermi Energy to the Diode

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Fermi-distribution lesson; the energy-band lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

The conduction electrons of a metal form a degenerate Fermi gas: they fill almost the whole Fermi sphere, and only a thin surface layer takes part in transport. A semiconductor tunes its carriers by doping; the depletion layer and built-in field of the pn junction give rectification — the heart of modern electronics.

E_F=ℏ²(3π²n)^(2/3)/(2m); I=I₀(e^(qV/k_BT)−1)

From assumptions to results

  1. At T=0 the electrons fill the sphere |k|≤k_F; the three-dimensional density of states g(E)∝√E gives E_F and the Fermi temperature T_F=E_F/k_B.
  2. At finite temperature only a thin layer within ~k_BT of the Fermi surface rearranges, and the electronic specific heat is ∝T.
  3. When the p and n regions come into contact the Fermi levels align, a depletion layer forms at the junction, forward bias lowers the barrier exponentially while the reverse current saturates, and the rectifier formula follows.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Copper’s n≈8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³ gives E_F≈7 eV and T_F≈8×10⁴ K, so room temperature is only a perturbation; silicon has about 10¹⁶ m⁻³ intrinsic carriers at room temperature, and doping 10²² m⁻³ donors raises the conductivity by about a factor of a million; at 0.7 V forward bias e^(qV/k_BT)≈e^28, so the current far exceeds the reverse current.

Range of applicability

Independent-particle approximation; strong correlations, heterojunctions, and ideality factors (1<n<2) require separate treatment.

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

Pauli blocking prevents deeply seated states from being excited; only the fraction ~T/T_F of electrons near the Fermi surface can absorb thermal energy.

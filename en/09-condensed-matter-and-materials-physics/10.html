The Classical Hall Effect and the Sign of the Carriers

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Lorentz force; Ohm’s law. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field develops a transverse Hall voltage whose sign is set by the carrier charge — the positive sign, once “contrary to classical expectations”, was precisely the evidence for conduction by holes; the Hall coefficient directly gives the carrier density and sign.

R_H=E_H/(jB)=1/(nq)

From assumptions to results

  1. In steady state the transverse electric force balances the Lorentz force, qE_H=qvB.
  2. Substituting j=nqv gives E_H=jB/(nq), so the Hall coefficient carries a sign.
  3. Negative sign = electrons; positive sign = holes (quasiparticles at the top of a filled band).
  4. The quantum Hall lesson extends the same geometry to two dimensions in a strong field.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

Copper has negative R_H; p-type semiconductors positive; a measured R_H=−7×10⁻¹¹ m³/C corresponds to n≈9×10²⁸ m⁻³, consistent with copper’s valence-electron density.

Range of applicability

A single-band model; multiband and strong-field cases require tensor transport analysis.

Independent calculation

Show solution

R_H=1/(nq); q>0 gives a positive coefficient.

Transfer and counterexample

Why is the Hall effect in semiconductors more pronounced than in metals?

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

The carrier density is several orders of magnitude lower, so R_H∝1/n is correspondingly larger and the measurement more sensitive.

Kerr Black Holes and Gravitational-Wave Detection

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation breaks down.

Prerequisites: the classical tests lesson; the concept of waves. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

A rotating black hole is described by the Kerr metric, its angular momentum dragging spacetime; accelerating masses radiate gravitational waves. With a Michelson interferometer plus Fabry–Pérot cavities, LIGO directly detected strains of 10⁻²¹ from binary black hole mergers, opening gravitational-wave astronomy.

Kerr a=J/(Mc)；r₊=M+√(M²−a²) (geometric units)

From assumptions to results

  1. Solving the axisymmetric vacuum field equations gives the Kerr metric; a→0 recovers Schwarzschild, while too large an a produces a naked singularity (the cosmic censorship conjecture).
  2. The quadrupole radiation formula gives the gravitational-wave power and orbital decay of binary stars (indirectly verified with the Hulse–Taylor binary; Nobel Prize 1993).
  3. Ground-based detectors operate above 10 Hz, with arm length and FP cavities amplifying the displacement signal.

Example and check

GW150914: 36+29 M☉ merged into 62 M☉, with about 3 M☉c² of energy released as gravitational waves within 0.2 s; the peak luminosity of about 3×10⁴⁹ W exceeds the combined luminosity of all the stars in the observable universe; the peak strain was 1.0×10⁻²¹.

Range of validity

Linear detection approximation; waveform modeling relies on numerical relativity.

Transfer and counterexample

Why do gravitational-wave detectors use kilometer-scale arms?

Check the transfer solution

Strain is a relative length change ΔL=hL, so longer arms give a larger absolute displacement, and FP cavities then amplify it several-hundred-fold.

Beam matrices and phase-space emittance

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation breaks down.

Prerequisites: matrices; phase space; covariance. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

Paraxial linear optics connects a particle's transverse position and slope through transport matrices. A beam is not a single trajectory but an ensemble distribution; second-order moments and their area scale describe the beam's spread and its phase-space quality.

Σ_out=MΣ_inMᵀ；ε=√detΣ

From assumptions to results

  1. The particle coordinate vector satisfies u_out=Mu_in.
  2. Averaging the outer product of the mean-subtracted vectors gives the covariance propagation formula.
  3. If detM=1, the determinant is preserved.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

The ideal drift matrix is [[1,L],[0,1]], which turns the position into x+Lx′; the distribution can widen, but under linear, lossless transport the geometric emittance does not increase as a result.

Limits of validity

Paraxial, linear, fixed reference momentum; nonlinearities, scattering, acceleration, and space charge require further treatment.

Independent calculation

Show solution

Lx′=1×0.002 m=2 mm; the final position is 1+2=3 mm.

Transfer and counterexample

Why does a smaller beam spot not automatically mean a smaller emittance?

Check the transfer-exercise solution

Focusing often trades a larger angular spread for a smaller position spread; the phase-space area can stay unchanged.

Overview / Nonlinear and Complex Systems / Lesson 11

Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Complex networks: from random graphs to small worlds

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the chaos lesson; statistical-physics concepts. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first go back to Learning Preparation. Two introductory lessons within the same field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Abstract a system into nodes and edges: phase transitions of random graphs, the high clustering and short paths of small worlds, the hubs of scale-free networks — epidemics, power grids, and brains all use the same statistical physics of networks.

mean degree ⟨k⟩; small world: high clustering + short paths

From assumptions to results

  1. The Erdős–Rényi random graph has a giant-component transition at ⟨k⟩=1.
  2. Watts–Strogatz randomly rewires a few edges of a regular ring, keeping clustering high while path lengths plummet (six degrees of separation).
  3. Barabási–Albert preferential attachment generates a power-law degree distribution P(k)∝k^(−3): hubs carry disproportionate connectivity.
  4. Epidemic thresholds, cascading failures, and synchronization (linking up with Kuramoto) are all determined by the spectrum and the topology.

Work out the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

The scale-free character of the Internet and metabolic networks makes them robust to random failures yet fragile to targeted attacks; cascading models of large power-grid blackouts; the small-world property of brain networks balances segregation with integration.

Limits of validity

Static-graph abstraction; temporal networks and hypergraphs are active extensions; be careful about causal direction.

Independent computation

Show solution

For ⟨k⟩>1 a giant component containing a finite fraction of the nodes appears abruptly.

Transfer and counterexample

Why are scale-free networks robust to random failures?

Show the transfer solution

Under a power-law degree distribution most nodes have few connections, so a random failure most likely hits a peripheral node and the bulk of the network stays connected.

Advanced self-test

This problem adds one step to the previous one or requires changing units; in the days-1, 3, and 7 reviews, do this problem instead to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

Removing hubs fragments the network rapidly, whereas random failures mostly hit peripheral nodes.

From learning knowledge to independent judgment

Introductory → Core → Advanced
→ Research training.

“Master” is not a rank that can be conferred for reading through the files. This site breaks the goal into abilities that can be checked, and advances by prerequisites rather than estimating your level by the number of pages.

Stage 0: Preparation

Study algebra, trigonometry, units, and graphs; then go on to make up calculus, complex numbers, linear algebra, and probability. This corresponds to Learning Preparation. Pass conditions: you can dimension-check physics formulas, explain derivatives and integrals, and solve second-order linear equations and simple eigenvalue problems.

Stage 1: Foundational physics

The order is classical mechanics → vibrations and waves → heat and electromagnetism → optics → special relativity and an introduction to quantum mechanics. Learn the phenomena and the experimental evidence first, then build the models. Pass conditions: you can independently draw free-body diagrams, keep an energy ledger, explain interactions with fields, and explain quantum measurement with probability.

Stage 2: Undergraduate core

Systematically make up analytical mechanics, vector electromagnetism, thermodynamics and statistical physics, and quantum mechanics; study mathematical methods, experimental measurement, and computation in parallel. The current undergraduate-core lessons in each field are still selected-topics entry points and cannot replace full semesters. You should read the corresponding chapters of the main textbooks, work the end-of-chapter problems, and have them graded.

Stage 3: Advanced specialization

Choose 1–2 fields to go deep in: for example, condensed matter needs many-body theory and statistical physics; particle physics needs relativistic quantum theory and field theory; gravity needs differential geometry; fluids need PDEs and stability. The current advanced selected-topic lessons demonstrate only some of the methods; other topics remain to be filled in on the taxonomy.

Stage 4: Research training and independent work

First reproduce known results, then compare models with data, read reviews and papers, and gradually shape new questions that can be tested. Pass conditions should include advisor or peer review, reproducible materials, and an accurate judgment of the limitations. There is no uniform timeline in which original research is guaranteed to be completed.

Arranging your training by learning goal

  1. Conceptual interest: go through the introductory lessons first and find two or three questions you want to dig into.
  2. University foundations: plan consecutive semesters around the four major theories; the short self-tests in this project alone are not enough.
  3. Research preparation: settle on a specific field, make up the prerequisites, complete reproduction projects, and get external feedback.

One possible week: 2 sessions of concepts and derivations, 2 of independent problem solving, 1 of experiment or computation, and 1 of closed-book review. Adjust the durations to your background; no personal assessment results are invented here.

Systematic courses and open resources

  • OpenStax University Physics: foundational textbooks with end-of-chapter problems.
  • MIT OCW 8.01SC: how a complete mechanics course organizes worked examples and exercises.
  • David Tong's lecture notes index: theoretical physics lecture notes from undergraduate to graduate level; read them following the prerequisites of each course.
  • arXiv category taxonomy: entry points for finding paper topics; for preprints, check the arguments and any subsequent peer review independently.

The external links require an internet connection; the current English short lessons and training guides can all be read offline.

The Fourier transform, convolution, and the δ function

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Fourier series lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, return first to Mathematics Preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

Extending the periodic expansion to non-periodic signals gives the Fourier transform: the time domain and the frequency domain are two representations of the same information. The convolution theorem exchanges convolution in a linear system with multiplication in the frequency domain, and the δ function is the identity element of this language.

F(ω)=∫f(t)e^(−iωt)dt；(f*g)(t)=∫f(τ)g(t−τ)dτ；f*g ↔ F·G

From assumptions to results

  1. Letting the period of the Fourier series go to infinity, the discrete spectrum densifies into a continuous transform, with the forward and inverse transforms inverting each other.
  2. A linear time-invariant system decomposes the input into a superposition of δ's; the output is the convolution of the input with the impulse response, which becomes a product in the transform domain.
  3. The δ is defined by the sifting property ∫f(t)δ(t−t₀)dt=f(t₀), and its spectrum is constant — all frequencies in equal measure.

Worked example and check

The transform of a Gaussian is again a Gaussian, with the widths in the two domains inversely related — precisely the mathematical skeleton of the uncertainty relation; a signal band-limited to B must be sampled at a rate above 2B to avoid aliasing (connecting to the Nyquist self-test).

Range of applicability

Square-integrable functions and slowly growing generalized functions; discrete sampling and finite windows introduce leakage and the picket-fence effect.

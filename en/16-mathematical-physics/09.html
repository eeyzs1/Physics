Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

The three classic equations: wave, heat conduction, and Laplace

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the separation-of-variables lesson; basic notions of PDEs. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, return first to Mathematics Preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

The three pillar equations of mathematical physics describe propagation (wave, second order in time), diffusion (heat conduction, first order in time), and equilibrium (Laplace, no time); the same separation-of-variables mechanism yields completely different spectral behavior.

∇²u=(1/c²)u_tt；u_t=α∇²u；∇²φ=0

From assumptions to results

  1. Force analysis of a string/membrane gives the wave equation, and separation of variables gives standing-wave eigenmodes (ω∝k).
  2. Substituting Fourier's law into energy conservation gives the heat equation, whose modes decay as e^(−αk²t) (long waves decay slowly).
  3. Source-free steady states give the Laplace equation, whose solutions are harmonic functions satisfying the mean-value property and uniqueness (linking to the electromagnetism field).

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

The eigenfrequencies of a string fixed at both ends are evenly spaced (harmonics), and the heat-equation decay rate is ∝n² (high-frequency spikes are smoothed out first); solutions of the Laplace equation have no interior extrema — extrema of the electrostatic potential can occur only on the boundary.

Range of applicability

Linear homogeneous media; nonlinear waves (shock waves) and variable coefficients require numerical methods.

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

The heat equation is parabolic; its long-tailed kernel produces an immediate tiny response at any distance — a nonrelativistic approximation; relativistic corrections require a hyperbolic formulation.

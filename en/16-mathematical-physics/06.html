Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Sturm–Liouville theory, orthogonality and completeness

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the separation-of-variables lesson; the special-functions lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, return first to Mathematics Preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

The eigenvalue problems arising in separation of variables are all written uniformly in Sturm–Liouville form; the eigenfunctions are automatically orthogonal with a weight and complete — Fourier, Legendre, Bessel, and Hermite are all special cases, and the boundary conditions decide whether the spectrum is discrete or continuous.

(py′)′+[q+λw]y=0；∫yₙyₘ·w dx=0（n≠m）

From assumptions to results

  1. Cross-multiply the equations for two eigenvalues, subtract, and integrate; the three types of boundary conditions make the endpoint terms vanish, giving weighted orthogonality.
  2. Completeness is guaranteed by the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators.
  3. Any function can be expanded in the eigenfunctions, with the coefficients given by orthogonality.
  4. The eigenvalues are real, and the eigenfunctions can be taken real.

Fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

Fourier series correspond to periodic boundary conditions (p=w=1); Legendre corresponds to p=1−x² with natural boundary conditions at ±1; the Laguerre functions for the hydrogen radial problem — one theorem unifies the legitimacy of every orthogonal expansion.

Range of applicability

Holds within the three types of well-posed boundary conditions; singular cases require the limit-point / limit-circle classification.

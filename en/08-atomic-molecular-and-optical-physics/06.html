The Stark Effect: Atomic Energy Levels in an Electric Field

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case on your own, and point out when the approximations break down.

Prerequisites: the perturbation theory lesson; the hydrogen atom lesson. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in the same field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

An external electric field shifts and splits atomic energy levels: the degeneracy of hydrogen with n≥2 is partly lifted by the field, producing the linear Stark effect; a nondegenerate ground state shows only a second-order (quadratic) effect — symmetry determines the order of the response.

Hydrogen n=2, linear ΔE=3ea₀E; second order ΔE=−½αE²

From assumptions to results

  1. The four degenerate states of hydrogen n=2, coupled by the field, are diagonalized in a parabolic-coordinate basis; the splitting is ΔE=3ea₀E·(n₁−n₂), and the linear behavior stems from the degeneracy.
  2. The ground state has no permanent dipole; the first-order matrix element vanishes by parity, and at second order a sum over intermediate states gives the polarizability and the energy shift −½αE².
  3. Compare with the harmonic oscillator: the linear term can be removed by a shift of coordinates.

Please fill in the intermediate algebra steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first point out the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Example and check

For hydrogen n=2 in E=10⁵ V/m: ΔE=3ea₀E ≈ 1.6×10⁻⁵ eV, comparable to fine structure; the ground-state polarizability of hydrogen is about 4.5 a.u., and the second-order shift is several orders of magnitude smaller still.

Range of applicability

Weak-field approximation (eEa₀≪ the level spacing); strong-field mixing and ionization require nonperturbative treatment.

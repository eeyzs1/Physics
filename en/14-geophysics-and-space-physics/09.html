Tides and the Earth's Rotation

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, independently compute a new case, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the gravitation lesson; rotation. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in the same field do not amount to satisfying all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The differential gravity of the Moon and the Sun stretches the Earth: ocean tides, solid-Earth tides, and friction feed the Earth's rotational angular momentum into the Moon's orbit — the day lengthens by about 2.3 ms per century, and the Moon recedes at 3.8 cm/yr; the tidal force falls off as the cube of the distance.

tidal acceleration ≈2GMR/d³; length of day +2.3 ms/century

From assumptions to results

  1. Taking the difference of the gravitational force across the Earth gives a tidal field ∝2GMR/d³ (the Moon's contribution exceeds the Sun's by a factor of about 2.2, even though the Sun's total gravitational pull is greater).
  2. The tidal bulge is dragged ahead of the Earth–Moon line by the rotation, and its torque transfers angular momentum from the Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit (lunar laser ranging directly confirms the recession).
  3. Conservation of angular momentum couples the recession to the slowing of the spin.
  4. Synchronous rotation (tidal locking) is the end state (the Moon is already locked).

Example and check

The lunar tidal acceleration is about 1.1×10⁻⁶ m/s² (differential gravity at the Earth's surface), with the Sun contributing about half; hundreds of millions of years ago the day was only about 22 hours (corroborated by coral growth bands); tidal locking explains why the Moon always keeps the same face toward the Earth.

Range of validity

Rigid-body plus equilibrium-tide approximation; shallow-sea resonances make the actual tidal range far exceed the equilibrium value.

Transfer and counterexample

Why does the Moon always keep the same face toward the Earth?

Solution to the transfer problem

Tidal friction has already locked the Moon's rotation into synchrony with its orbit (the end state of the spin–orbit resonance).

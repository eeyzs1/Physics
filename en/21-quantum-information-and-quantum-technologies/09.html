Quantum key distribution and BB84

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: qubits; measurement. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The quantum no-cloning theorem guarantees that eavesdropping inevitably disturbs the quantum states, giving key distribution physical security. BB84 encodes randomly in two bases; comparing error rates afterwards estimates the extent of eavesdropping.

eavesdropping introduces a 25% error rate；security threshold about 11% (one-way error correction)

From assumptions to results

  1. Sender and receiver choose bases at random and keep the bits encoded in the same basis.
  2. An eavesdropper measuring in a random basis picks the wrong basis with probability 1/2 and then flips the result with probability 1/2, introducing a 25% error rate in total.
  3. Privacy amplification squeezes out the leakage.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked examples and checks

With 1000 detected photons and an error rate of 3%<11%, several hundred bits of secure key can still be obtained after error correction and privacy amplification; if the error rate exceeds the threshold, the round is discarded and sent again.

Limits of applicability

An ideal single-photon source; side-channel loopholes in real devices require device-independent protocols as a defense.

Qubits: from amplitudes to measurement

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: complex numbers; probability; two-dimensional vectors. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

A pure state of a qubit is described by two complex amplitudes, and normalization guarantees that the measurement probabilities sum to 1. Geometrically, a pure state corresponds to a point on the Bloch sphere: the north pole |0〉, the south pole |1〉, and the equator the equal-weight superpositions; the latitude gives the populations and the longitude gives the relative phase. Looking only at computational-basis probabilities loses the phase information.

|ψ〉=α|0〉+β|1〉；|α|²+|β|²=1

From assumptions to results

  1. A computational-basis measurement uses the squared modulus of the projection.
  2. The Hadamard gate takes |0〉 to (|0〉+|1〉)/√2, turning phase information into a population difference in another basis.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked examples and checks

|+〉 and |−〉 both give probabilities of 1/2 each in the computational basis; after H is applied they become |0〉 and |1〉 respectively, so they can be distinguished deterministically.

Limits of applicability

Real quantum devices have mixed states, noise, and readout errors; the pure-state vector is only an idealized description.

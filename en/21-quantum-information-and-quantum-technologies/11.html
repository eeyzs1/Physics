A first look at quantum algorithms: Deutsch–Jozsa and Grover

Goal: follow the derivations to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the quantum gates lesson; superposition and interference. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Quantum parallelism is not "computing all answers at once": interference funnels a global property (constancy, searchability) into a single measurement. Deutsch–Jozsa decides whether a function is constant or balanced with one query, and Grover gives a quadratic speedup for unstructured search — definitive examples of quantum advantage.

Grover：amplitude amplification per iteration ∝√(1/N)，number of iterations ~π√N/4

From assumptions to results

  1. Encode the function values into a phase oracle (U_f|x〉=±|x〉); Hadamard spreads the initial state into a uniform superposition; after the query, do not measure — use Hadamard/phase inversion to make the amplitudes interfere and concentrate on the target state.
  2. Deutsch determines the property of the function with a single query (classically two are needed).
  3. Each Grover iteration rotates the answer amplitude by a small angle; after about √N iterations it lines up with the measurement basis.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce one, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked examples and checks

N=10⁶ entries: classically 5×10⁵ queries on average, with Grover about π√(10⁶)/4≈785; the quadratic speedup is significant but does not turn NP-complete problems into polynomial time.

Limits of applicability

Coherent oracles and fault-tolerant hardware are required; in the NISQ era only small-scale demonstrations are possible.

Transfer and counterexamples

Why can't quantum parallelism read out all the function values directly?

Compare with the transfer solution

A measurement yields only one basis-vector outcome; the algorithm must use interference to funnel the global property of interest into a single measurement — reading out all the values is forbidden by the measurement postulate.

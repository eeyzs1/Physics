Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Exoplanet detection: transits, radial velocities, and microlensing

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the distance-ladder lesson; photometry. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, return to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The three main methods of finding planets each have their strengths: radial velocities measure mass, transits measure radius and atmosphere, and microlensing finds cold planets at large distances; thousands of planets are confirmed, and the occurrence-rate spectrum shows that "super-Earths" are more common than hot Jupiters.

Transit depth ΔF=(R_p/R_★)²; radial-velocity amplitude K∝M_p sin i

From assumptions to result

  1. Transits: the planet blocks the star, dimming the light by (R_p/R_★)², and the repetition period gives the orbit.
  2. Radial velocity: the star's Doppler wobble around the barycenter gives M_p sin i.
  3. Microlensing: the planet briefly amplifies the gravitational lensing by a foreground star (linking to the gravitational-lensing lesson).
  4. Combining transit and radial-velocity data gives the density and hence the composition.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and cross-check

Earth's transit depth across the Sun is about 0.008% (84 ppm), Jupiter's about 1%; Kepler confirmed thousands of planets by transits; TRAPPIST-1 has seven Earth-sized planets (an M8 red dwarf, with markedly larger transit depths).

Boundaries of applicability

Detection is biased toward short periods and close-in orbits; atmospheric characterization requires transit spectroscopy.

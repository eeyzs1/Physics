Stellar structure and the main sequence

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: gravity; conservation of energy; thermal radiation. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, return to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

A star is a self-gravitating sphere of gas: hydrostatic equilibrium, nuclear burning, and radiative/convective transport jointly determine its structure. The main sequence is the long-lived stable state of core hydrogen burning, with luminosity rising steeply with mass.

Hydrostatics dP/dr=−Gm(r)ρ/r²; main-sequence L∝M^3.5 (approximate)

From assumptions to result

  1. Write out the pressure balance shell by shell.
  2. Matching the energy generation rate to the luminosity gives the energy equation.
  3. Radiative diffusion or convection sets the temperature gradient.
  4. Solving the equations together requires numerical integration. L∝M^3.5 is a fit to main-sequence models: an order-of-magnitude derivation is possible under power-law assumptions such as κ∝constant and ε∝T^ν (a uniform-star model gives L∝M³ to order of magnitude), but it cannot be derived analytically from the listed equations alone.
  5. At too-high luminosity there is also the Eddington luminosity limit L_Edd=4πGMm_pc/σ_T.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first point out the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Stuck? Show the key intermediate steps
  1. Order-of-magnitude derivation for a uniform star: central pressure p_c~GM²/R⁴, central temperature T_c~GMm_p/(kR).
  2. Luminosity from the radiative diffusion approximation: L~4πR²·(4σT_c⁴)/(3κρR·C).
  3. Substitute the mass–radius relation R∝M^(1/2) (a main-sequence approximation) to eliminate R.
  4. This gives L∝M³ to order of magnitude.
  5. With κ∝T^aρ^b and ε∝T^ν, dimensional homogeneity gives L∝M^ξ, with ξ between 3 and 4.
  6. Numerical integration of the full equations (including convection) gives the empirical 3.5.

If you are still stuck, write down the specific equation and then discuss it; that is more effective than rereading repeatedly.

Worked example and cross-check

The Sun's luminosity is 3.8×10²⁶ W and its main-sequence lifetime is about ten billion years; a star of 10 times the mass has about 3000 times the luminosity, and its lifetime shrinks to about one three-hundredth.

Boundaries of applicability

Main-sequence approximation; massive stars have strong stellar winds, and low-mass stars are fully convective.

Compare with the transfer-problem solution

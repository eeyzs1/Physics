Stellar pulsation and Cepheid variables

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: basic ideas of radiative transport; sound waves. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, return to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

In some stars, the helium ionization zone in the outer layers makes the opacity increase under compression, producing self-sustaining radial pulsation (the κ mechanism); the period of Cepheid variables increases monotonically with luminosity — the Cepheid period–luminosity relation is the gold-standard ruler of the distance ladder.

Pulsation approximation P≈2π√(R³/GM) (acoustic scale); period–luminosity relation M≈−2.8logP−1.4

From assumptions to result

  1. When it contracts and heats up, the helium ionization zone absorbs more radiation and blocks the flow; heat accumulates, the star re-expands, and the cycle sustains itself as a limit cycle.
  2. The pulsation period is set by the sound-crossing time √(R³/GM); larger stars are brighter and slower — hence the monotonic period–luminosity relation.
  3. Once calibrated, Cepheids measure galaxy distances (Hubble used them to identify galaxies beyond our own).

Worked example and cross-check

Classical Cepheids have P=1–100 days and M≈−2 to −6; the period–luminosity slope is about −2.8: doubling the period raises the luminosity by a factor of about 2.2; Delta Cephei (4.6 days) is the prototype.

Boundaries of applicability

The period–luminosity relation requires metallicity calibration; nonradial pulsators and RR Lyrae stars have their own rulers.

Why are Cepheids "standard candles" while ordinary stars are not?

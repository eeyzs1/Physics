Inferring celestial objects from the light we receive

Lesson goal: be able to explain "inferring celestial objects from the light we receive" in your own words, and complete the calculations and conditions-of-validity judgments below independently.

Prerequisites: mechanics; thermal physics; optics; relativity needed for the advanced parts. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

First, think about a question

If the distance to a source of fixed luminosity triples, what fraction of the original flux do we receive? Note your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your reasoning changed.

Understanding the model

Celestial objects are far away; what we receive directly are observables such as spectra, brightness, and angular position. If absorption is neglected and radiation is assumed isotropic, the luminosity spreads over a sphere of radius equal to the distance, so the received flux falls off with the inverse square of the distance.

F = L/(4πd²)

Order of use: identify the object of study and the conditions → make the units consistent → write down the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The specific meanings and conventions of the variables are given in the worked example below.

Work through the example with reasoning

If the distance to the same star doubles, the received flux becomes 1/4. A dimmer appearance may come from greater distance or from a lower intrinsic luminosity; more information is needed to distinguish the two.

Check the boundary cases

Apparent brightness and luminosity are different things; you cannot judge a star's temperature or distance from dimness alone.

Look back at the example: name at least one neglected factor and explain under what circumstances it could affect the result. After one condition is changed, does the original formula still apply?

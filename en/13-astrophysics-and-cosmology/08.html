Binary stars and mass determination

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Kepler lesson; the Doppler effect. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, return to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Most stars in the Universe come in pairs: Kepler's third law gives the total mass from the orbital period and separation, and radial-velocity curves then split it between the two stars — this is the fundamental method for measuring stellar masses and also a means of identifying black hole candidates.

P²=4π²a³/[G(m₁+m₂)]

From assumptions to result

  1. The Newtonian form of Kepler's third law ties the orbital period, separation, and total mass together.
  2. The two radial-velocity curves of a double-lined binary give the mass ratio q=m₂/m₁.
  3. The light curve of an eclipsing binary gives the orbital inclination, and solving together yields the individual masses.
  4. In X-ray binaries the companion's orbit fixes the compact object's mass (black hole candidates 5–20 M☉).

Worked example and cross-check

For a binary with total mass 2 M☉ and separation 1 AU: P=1/√2 yr≈0.707 yr; the Sirius binary (P≈50 yr) gives a white-dwarf companion of about 1.0 M☉, close to the Chandrasekhar limit (connection to the stellar-remnants lesson).

Boundaries of applicability

Point-mass approximation; tidal effects and mass transfer in close binaries require corrections.

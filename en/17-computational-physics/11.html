Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Iterative solution of large linear systems: Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, and multigrid

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation breaks down.

Prerequisites: the Poisson-equation lesson; matrix iteration. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two intro lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

A Poisson equation on a million-point grid cannot be factorized directly: basic iterations (Jacobi/GS) act like low-pass filters that damp only the high-frequency error, while multigrid moves the low frequencies onto coarse grids to solve them, reaching near-linear complexity — the standard answer for large-scale elliptic problems.

iterative convergence rate ρ; multigrid work O(N)

From assumptions to results

  1. Jacobi updates synchronously, Gauss–Seidel updates in sequential order (about twice as fast), and SOR uses weighted extrapolation with the optimum ω≈2/(1+√(1−ρ²)).
  2. A high/low-frequency decomposition of the error shows that smoothing iterations grind down the high frequencies while the low frequencies barely move.
  3. Restrict the residual to a coarse grid, solve there, then interpolate back as a correction (V-cycle); coarse grids are exactly the ones good at low frequencies — the two levels complement each other.

Worked example and check

A Poisson equation on a 256³ grid: direct factorization is infeasible, conjugate gradient needs thousands of iterations, while a dozen-odd multigrid V-cycles converge to machine precision; large-scale solves in engineering electromagnetics, electrostatics, and gravitational potentials all use this as their backbone.

Limits of applicability

It requires the coarse-grid correction to genuinely reduce the low frequencies (rough coefficients need algebraic multigrid); nonlinear problems use FAS.

Why can't Jacobi iteration "grind down" low-frequency error?

Transfer-problem solution for comparison

Low-frequency error is nearly the same at neighboring points, so the averaging operation barely moves it; high frequencies are quickly wiped out by averaging — which is exactly why coarse grids are needed.

Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

FFT and numerical spectral analysis

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation breaks down.

Prerequisites: the Fourier-transform lesson; the concept of sampling. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two intro lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

The discrete Fourier transform maps N data points onto N frequencies; FFT uses divide-and-conquer to cut the operation count from N² to N·log₂N, making spectral analysis, fast convolution, and Poisson solvers practical across the board — the single most used algorithm in numerical computing.

X_k=Σ_n x_n·e^(−2πikn/N); complexity O(N·log₂N)

From assumptions to results

  1. Split the DFT by even and odd indices into two N/2-point DFTs; the recursion unfolds into log₂N levels, each doing O(N) butterfly merges.
  2. Lengths that are not powers of 2 use mixed radix or the Chirp-z transform.
  3. The inverse transform differs only by scaling and conjugation.

Worked example and check

For N=10⁶ points: a direct DFT takes about 10¹² complex multiplications, FFT about 2×10⁷ — roughly fifty thousand times faster; 44.1 kHz audio with N=4096 frames allows hundreds of spectral analyses per second; for N≳64 FFT convolution is already faster than direct convolution.

Limits of applicability

The periodic-extension assumption; frequency resolution Δf = sampling rate/N, and zero-padding only interpolates without adding information.

Transfer and counterexample

Why can't increasing the FFT size improve frequency resolution without limit?

Transfer-problem solution for comparison

Zero-padding only refines the frequency-domain grid without adding information; the true resolution is set by the data duration T, with Δf≈1/T.

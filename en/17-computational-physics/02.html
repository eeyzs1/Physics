How to trust a simulation result

Lesson goal: be able to explain "how to trust a simulation result" in your own words and complete the calculations and validity-condition checks below independently.

Prerequisites: calculus; basic programming; classical mechanics. If the formulas are hard to follow for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

Start with a question

In a first-order method, reducing the step size to 1/4 shrinks the expected error to 1/n of its original value: what is n? Note your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understanding the model

A numerical result should at least be compared against analytical special cases, step-size convergence, and independent physical laws. For a first-order method, once inside the asymptotic convergence regime, halving the step size usually halves the global error; rounding error and model error may still be present.

error ≈ C·Δt (first-order global error model)

Order of use: identify the system and conditions → make the units consistent → write down the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The precise meaning and conventions of the variables are given in the worked example below.

Worked example with reasoning

If the error is 0.08 at step size 0.1, then at step size 0.05 inside the asymptotic regime the expectation is about 0.04. In practice you should keep comparing several step sizes, rather than treating one pair of close results as proof.

Check the boundary cases

Stability is not the same as accuracy; a stable but overly coarse computation can still lead to wrong conclusions.

Look back at the worked example: name at least one neglected factor and explain under what circumstances it could affect the result. After changing one condition, does the original formula still apply?

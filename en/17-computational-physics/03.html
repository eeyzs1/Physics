Why the symplectic Euler method suits oscillators better

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation breaks down.

Prerequisites: numerical integration; Hamilton's equations. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two intro lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

The plain explicit Euler method updates using the old position and the old momentum at the same time, which can cause the energy of an oscillator to drift steadily. Symplectic methods preserve the geometric structure of phase space and often keep the long-term energy error a bounded oscillation, but they do not guarantee exact energy conservation at every step.

p_(n+1)=p_n−kq_nΔt; q_(n+1)=q_n+p_(n+1)Δt/m

From assumptions to results

  1. Update the momentum with the old position first, then update the position with the new momentum.
  2. The composition of the two shear maps has determinant 1 and is symplectic with respect to the canonical structure.

Worked example and check

With m = k = 1, q₀ = 1, p₀ = 0, Δt = 0.1, one gets p₁ = −0.1 and q₁ = 0.99; the energy after the first step is about 0.49505 rather than exactly 0.5.

Limits of applicability

Stability still requires a suitable step size; dissipative systems, stiff equations, and constrained systems may need other algorithms.

Independent calculation

Show solution

p₁ = −0.2, q₁ = 1 + (−0.2) × 0.2 = 0.96.

Transfer and counterexample

Why can't "area-preserving" be directly equated with "energy-preserving"?

Transfer-problem solution for comparison

One constrains the local phase-space volume or the symplectic form, the other constrains the value of the energy function; the two are different — a symplectic method can preserve the geometry while the energy carries a small error.

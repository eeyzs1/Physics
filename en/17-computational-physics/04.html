Finite differences and the Poisson equation

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation breaks down.

Prerequisites: numerical integration; partial derivatives. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two intro lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

Discretize a continuous field onto a grid and turn derivatives into differences, and the elliptic Poisson equation becomes a sparse linear system — the standard numerical solution method for electrostatics, gravitation, and steady-state diffusion.

∇²φ≈(φᵢ₊₁ⱼ+φᵢ₋₁ⱼ+φᵢⱼ₊₁+φᵢⱼ₋₁−4φᵢⱼ)/h²

From assumptions to results

  1. A Taylor expansion yields the second-order central difference.
  2. The five-point stencil gives a symmetric sparse matrix.
  3. Jacobi or Gauss–Seidel iteration converges to the discrete solution, with overall truncation error O(h²).

Please fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

Iterating the electrostatic potential on a 100×100 grid until the residual reaches 10⁻⁶ gives O(h²) accuracy; halving the mesh and verifying that the error shrinks by about a factor of 4 confirms the order of convergence.

Limits of applicability

Regular grids; complex geometries and higher-order accuracy require finite element or spectral methods.

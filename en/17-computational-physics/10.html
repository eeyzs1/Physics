Richardson extrapolation and convergence acceleration

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation breaks down.

Prerequisites: the step-size convergence lesson; Taylor expansions. If the prerequisites feel shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two intro lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and physical picture

If you know the power-law behavior of the error, you can combine results at two step sizes to cancel the leading error term: Richardson extrapolation lifts O(h²) to O(h⁴) or higher — a free lunch in numerical accuracy.

A(h)=A₀+c·h²+O(h⁴); combination [4A(h/2)−A(h)]/3

From assumptions to results

  1. Assume the discrete result carries an error series A(h)=A₀+c₁h^p+….
  2. Take a linear combination of the results at h and h/2, choosing coefficients that cancel the h^p term.
  3. Applying it repeatedly yields the Romberg integration table, gaining two orders per level.
  4. The even-function error structure of the trapezoidal rule is exactly what permits this expansion.

Please fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

Integrating sin with the trapezoidal rule at h and h/2 gives an error ratio of about 4:1 (second-order verification), and one extrapolation reaches fourth order; a few rows of the Romberg table push the accuracy to near machine precision; extrapolation is likewise used for differential equations and extrapolated stopping criteria.

Limits of applicability

It requires the error to truly follow an asymptotic power law; rounding error rebounds after excessive extrapolation.

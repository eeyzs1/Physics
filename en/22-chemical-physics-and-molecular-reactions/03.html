Transition-State Theory and Activation Rates

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: reaction rates; the Boltzmann factor. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

A reaction must cross the saddle point of the potential energy surface (the transition state). The rate constant is proportional to the Boltzmann probability of reaching the saddle point times the crossing frequency along the reaction coordinate; hence the exponential temperature dependence.

k≈(kBT/h)e^(−Ea/RT) (Eyring form)

From Assumptions to Results

  1. Assume quasi-equilibrium between the reactants and the transition state.
  2. The frequency of motion along the reaction coordinate is about kBT/h.
  3. The ratio of partition functions gives the pre-exponential factor, and the activation enthalpy gives the exponential factor.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked Example and Check

For Ea=50 kJ/mol at 300 K: kBT/h≈6×10¹² s⁻¹ and the exponential e^(−20.0)≈2×10⁻⁹, so k≈10⁴ s⁻¹; heated to 310 K the exponent becomes e^(−19.4), and the rate increases about 1.9-fold.

Limits of Applicability

Assumes transition-state quasi-equilibrium with no tunneling or recrossing; light-atom systems at low temperature need quantum corrections.

Independent Calculation

Show solution

The exponent goes from −20.0 to −19.4, and the ratio is e^(0.65)≈1.9.

Transfer and Counterexample

If Ea doubles to 100 kJ/mol, what factor does the same temperature rise give?

Show the transfer-exercise solution

e^(1.3)≈3.7 times.

Advanced Self-Test

One step more than the previous problem, or a unit change; on review days 1, 3, and 7 do this one instead, to avoid memorizing answers.

Show solution

Ea/R(1/300−1/310)=1.29, and e^1.29≈3.7.

  2. Find a case beyond the limits of applicability and determine which term of the formula fails first.
  3. Within two days, redo both self-tests without looking at the solutions; for each wrong step, return to the corresponding section.

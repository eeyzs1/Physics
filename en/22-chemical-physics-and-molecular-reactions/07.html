From Hartree–Fock to DFT: The Two Main Methods of Electronic Structure

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: concepts of quantum many-electron systems; the idea of the variational method. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

The many-electron Schrödinger equation is exponentially complex and cannot be solved directly. Hartree–Fock does a variational calculation with a single Slater determinant, so exchange effects are included automatically; DFT replaces the basic variable with the electron density, and its accuracy-to-cost ratio makes it the workhorse of materials science and chemistry.

HF: variational over a Slater determinant; DFT: E=E[ρ] (HK theorem)

From Assumptions to Results

  1. The wave function of 3 electrons depends on 9 spatial coordinates, while the density depends on only 3—the feasibility of the density as the variable is guaranteed by the Hohenberg–Kohn theorem (the ground-state density uniquely determines ground-state properties).
  2. Kohn–Sham maps the interacting problem onto an equivalent noninteracting system; the unknown exchange–correlation functional is the key to accuracy.
  3. HF neglects correlation and DFT approximates the XC functional; the two serve as benchmarks for each other.

Worked Example and Check

Typical orders of magnitude: an exact HF calculation misses the helium atom's energy by about 1% because HF lacks correlation energy; modern hybrid functionals reach errors at the level of chemical accuracy (about 4 kJ/mol) for molecular enthalpies of formation; worldwide, DFT is run millions of times a day for materials and drug screening.

Limits of Applicability

Single-reference methods fail in strongly correlated systems (Mott insulators, bond dissociation); multi-reference approaches and DMFT are required.

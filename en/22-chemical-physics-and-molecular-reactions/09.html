Intermolecular Forces: Dispersion, Dipoles, and Hydrogen Bonds

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the chemical-bonding lesson; concepts of electrostatics. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

The weak interactions between molecules come in layers: permanent dipole–dipole (orientation), induced dipole (Debye), and the dispersion force arising from instantaneous fluctuations (London), which is universal and ∝1/R⁶; the hydrogen bond is a strongly directional, short-lived "special liaison"—the backbone of water and of life.

London dispersion ∝−C₆/R⁶; hydrogen bond 10–40 kJ/mol

From Assumptions to Results

  1. Quantum fluctuations of a molecule create an instantaneous dipole that induces a dipole in its neighbor; second-order perturbation theory gives −C₆/R⁶ (C₆ is set by the polarizabilities and excitation energies).
  2. Thermal motion suppresses the dipole orientation average (at high temperature, ∝1/T).
  3. The hydrogen bond X–H···Y is part electrostatic and partly covalent, strongly directional, with a bond energy of about 0.1–0.4 eV.
  4. At shorter range repulsion takes over as R⁻¹² or exponential, combining into the Lennard-Jones potential (links to the MD lesson).

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked Example and Check

Water's anomalies (boiling point, surface tension, maximum density at 4 °C) spring from the hydrogen-bond network; a gecko's foot sticks by van der Waals forces (nanonewton-level forces per seta add up to a considerable total); graphite layers are held together by dispersion—exfoliating graphene means overcoming exactly that.

Limits of Applicability

The pairwise additivity approximation fails for strongly polar systems; hydrogen bonding in water must be treated as a network.

Independent Calculation

Show solution

10–40 kJ/mol ≈ 0.1–0.4 eV, typically 0.2.

Transfer and Counterexample

Why do noble gases (which have no dipole) liquefy as well?

Show the transfer-exercise solution

Dispersion forces come from instantaneous fluctuating dipoles and require no permanent dipole—argon liquefies at 87 K thanks to them.

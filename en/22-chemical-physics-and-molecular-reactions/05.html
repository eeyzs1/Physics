Chemical Bonding and Molecular Orbitals

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the hydrogen atom; identical particles. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

Atomic orbitals combine linearly into molecular orbitals: bonding orbitals are lowered in energy and antibonding orbitals raised; bond order=(bonding electrons−antibonding electrons)/2. Both the stability of H₂ and the paramagnetism of O₂ are explained directly by the orbital occupations.

bond order=(N_bonding−N_antibonding)/2

From Assumptions to Results

  1. Two atomic orbitals combine into two molecular orbitals, one bonding and one antibonding; the splitting is set by the overlap.
  2. Electrons fill according to the Pauli principle and Hund's rules; the net stabilization gives the bond length and bond energy.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked Example and Check

H₂ has bond order 1; in He₂ the bonding and antibonding orbitals are both filled, the bond order is 0, and the molecule cannot exist stably; the two unpaired π* electrons of O₂ explain its paramagnetism—a classic success of molecular orbital theory.

Limits of Applicability

Simple LCAO picture; delocalized systems and metallic bonding require a band description.

Independent Calculation

Show solution

After the valence electrons fill in, there are 8 bonding and 4 antibonding electrons, (8−4)/2=2.

Transfer and Counterexample

Why can He₂ not form a stable bond?

Show the transfer-exercise solution

Both the bonding and antibonding orbitals are filled, so the net stabilization is zero.

Femtochemistry: Seeing the Transition State

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the transition-state lesson; the pump–probe concept. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

The transition state of a chemical reaction exists for only about a hundred femtoseconds: femtosecond laser pump–probe photography records bond breaking and bond formation directly along the time axis—Zewail's femtochemistry (1999 Nobel Prize) turned the "transition state" from a concept into a movie you can watch.

pump–probe Δt scan; bond vibration period ~10⁻¹⁴ s

From Assumptions to Results

  1. A pump pulse triggers the reaction, and a probe pulse samples it after a controlled delay (absorption/ionization/diffraction).
  2. Scanning the delay reconstructs the evolution of the reaction coordinate in time.
  3. Bond oscillation periods are tens of femtoseconds, so hundred-femtosecond pulses suffice to resolve them.
  4. Transition-state absorption spectra, anharmonic decay, and coherent nucleation can all be filmed.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked Example and Check

The ICN → I + CN dissociation completes within ~200 fs; the cis–trans isomerization of retinal in the retina takes 200 fs (the first event of vision); femtosecond electron diffraction films bond motion directly; attosecond pulses push time resolution down to electron motion (2023 Nobel Prize).

Limits of Applicability

The pump must not disturb the system (weak excitation with many averages); molecular beams and ultrafast electron diffraction are complementary.

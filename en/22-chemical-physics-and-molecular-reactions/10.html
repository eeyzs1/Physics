Statistical Mechanics of Chemical Equilibrium: From Partition Functions to K_eq

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the partition-function lesson; the free-energy lesson. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

The chemical equilibrium constant is not a new law: compute ΔG° from the molecular partition functions of reactants and products, then get the equilibrium constant from ΔG°=−RT·ln K—statistical mechanics glues the tables of chemical thermodynamics together all the way down.

ΔG°=−RT·ln K; K follows from a ratio of partition functions

From Assumptions to Results

  1. Each molecule's translational/rotational/vibrational/electronic partition functions (linking to the heat-capacity ladder lesson) give the standard molar free energy.
  2. For the reaction, ΔG°=ΣνG°.
  3. ΔG°=−RT lnK turns the statistical quantities into a macroscopic equilibrium.
  4. The van 't Hoff equation d lnK/dT=ΔH°/RT² describes the temperature dependence (endothermic reactions are favored by heating).

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked Example and Check

At 298 K, ΔG°=−5.7 kJ/mol corresponds to K=10 (equilibrium strongly favors the products); for ammonia synthesis, K grows as the temperature falls (exothermic), and industry uses high pressure to make up for the slow room-temperature kinetics (the Haber process).

Limits of Applicability

Standard states of ideal gases/dilute solutions; activity corrections must be added.

Independent Calculation

Show solution

−ΔG°/RT=5700/2477≈2.3, and K=e^2.3≈10.

Transfer and Counterexample

Why does heating an exothermic reaction decrease K instead?

Show the transfer-exercise solution

The van 't Hoff equation d lnK/dT=ΔH/RT²: with ΔH<0 the slope is negative, so heating pushes the equilibrium down.

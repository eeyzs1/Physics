Molecular Vibration and Infrared Spectroscopy

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the harmonic oscillator; reduced mass. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

Molecular vibrations absorb infrared light: only modes accompanied by a change in dipole moment are IR-active; line positions give bond force constants, and the fingerprint region identifies molecules—a basic tool of chemical analysis.

ν̄=(1/2πc)√(k/μ)

From Assumptions to Results

  1. In the harmonic approximation, the reduced mass and the force constant determine the frequency.
  2. The selection rule requires the derivative of the dipole moment with respect to displacement to be nonzero.
  3. Anharmonicity produces overtone bands.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked Example and Check

For a C–H bond with k≈500 N/m and μ≈0.94 u: ν̄=√(k/μ)/(2πc)≈3000 cm⁻¹, right in the C–H stretching region; the 667 cm⁻¹ bending mode of CO₂ is a key greenhouse absorption.

Limits of Applicability

Harmonic and rigid-rotor approximations; large-amplitude anharmonicity and coupling require corrections.

Independent Calculation

Show solution

√(500/1.56×10⁻²⁷)/(2π×3×10¹⁰)≈3000 cm⁻¹.

Transfer and Counterexample

Why does O₂ have no infrared absorption?

Show the transfer-exercise solution

Vibration of a homonuclear diatomic molecule does not change the dipole moment, so it is IR-inactive.

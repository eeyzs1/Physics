Surface Catalysis and the Sabatier Principle

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: activation energy and rates; chemical bonding. If a prerequisite feels shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not amount to meeting every advanced prerequisite.

Problem and Physical Picture

A catalyst surface provides a low-barrier pathway: if adsorption is too weak the reactants are not activated, if too strong the products hardly desorb—activity versus adsorption energy forms a volcano shape with its peak at intermediate strength.

rate ∝θ(1−θ); θ=KP/(1+KP)

From Assumptions to Results

  1. Langmuir adsorption gives the relation between coverage and pressure.
  2. The reaction rate is proportional to an "occupied × vacant" type of product, maximal at intermediate coverage.
  3. The d-band center model relates adsorption strength to electronic structure.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first identify the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked Example and Check

In the iron catalyst for ammonia synthesis, N₂ dissociation is the rate-limiting step; the hydrogenation activity of transition metals follows a volcano curve in adsorption energy, with metals near the peak the most active.

Limits of Applicability

Ideal flat-surface model; real catalysts involve supports, defects, and nanosize effects.

Independent Calculation

Show solution

0.5×0.5=0.25, right at the volcano peak.

Transfer and Counterexample

Why is "strongest adsorption" not "best catalysis"?

Show the transfer-exercise solution

Products desorb with difficulty and active sites are blocked, so activity falls instead.

Boundary layers and flow separation

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: Poiseuille flow; viscosity. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

At high Reynolds number, viscous effects concentrate in a thin layer hugging the wall, within which the velocity rises from zero to the external-flow value. An adverse pressure gradient drives the slow near-wall fluid backward, the boundary layer separates, and a wake and pressure drag form.

δ≈(νx/U)^(1/2) (flat-plate laminar flow); separation point: zero wall shear stress

From assumptions to results

  1. Matching the orders of magnitude of viscous and inertial terms inside the thin layer gives δ∝√x.
  2. Under an adverse pressure gradient the near-wall fluid lacks the kinetic energy to keep advancing against the pressure, and separation occurs where τ_w=0.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

For a water flow with U=1 m/s (ν=10⁻⁶), δ≈1 mm at x=1 m; on an airfoil at a high angle of attack the adverse pressure gradient on the upper surface triggers separation, causing stall.

Limits of validity

These are flat-plate laminar-flow estimates; turbulent boundary layers grow faster but resist separation better; the separation point often has to be determined experimentally or by CFD.

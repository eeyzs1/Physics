Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Vorticity, circulation, and surface tension: the topology and surfaces of fluids

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the concept of vorticity; Kelvin's theorem. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

The vorticity ω=∇×v measures the local rotation of the fluid; in inviscid barotropic flow, conservation of circulation (Kelvin's theorem) freezes vortex tubes into the fluid — the Kutta–Joukowski theorem for aircraft lift follows from this. Surface tension, in turn, dominates small-scale phenomena at the water surface.

Γ=∮v·dl; Kelvin: DΓ/Dt=0 (inviscid, barotropic); capillary rise h=2γcosθ/(ρgr)

From assumptions to results

  1. Take the material derivative of the velocity circulation; conservation follows when the viscous and baroclinic terms vanish.
  2. A vortex tube cannot begin or end inside the fluid; it can only close into a loop or attach to a wall (smoke rings).
  3. The lift L′=ρUΓ follows once the circulation is fixed by the trailing-edge condition.
  4. Surface tension comes from molecular attraction across the interface; the capillary rise height is proportional to γ, with γ≈0.072 N/m for water.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

When flow starts past an airfoil, the trailing-edge vortex sheds and leaves behind a starting vortex of equal and opposite strength; conservation of circulation then requires the circulation around the wing to be established, giving lift L′=ρUΓ; in a fully wetting capillary of r=0.1 mm, water rises h=2γ/(ρgr)≈0.14 m.

Limits of validity

Inviscid, no-penetration model; boundary layers, vorticity diffusion, and the Magnus effect in real fluids require viscous corrections.

Independent computation

Show solution

h=2×0.072/(1000×10×10⁻⁴)=0.144 m=144 mm.

Transfer and counterexample

Why can a vortex tube not just "begin out of nowhere" inside the fluid?

Compare with the transfer-exercise solution

In inviscid flow, vortex lines move with the fluid (frozen in), and mathematically ∇·ω=0 forbids free ends — they can only close into loops, extend to a boundary, or terminate at a free surface.

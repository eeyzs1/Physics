Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Bernoulli's principle and flow energy

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: Reynolds number; conservation of energy. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

In steady, inviscid, incompressible flow along a streamline, the sum of the pressure, the dynamic pressure, and the elevation head is conserved: where the flow speed is high, the pressure is low. This is the statement of energy conservation along a streamline, not a universal law.

p+½ρv²+ρgz=constant (along a streamline)

From assumptions to results

  1. For steady inviscid flow, integrating Euler's equation along a streamline turns the inertial term into the gradient of ½v² and yields Bernoulli's equation.
  2. Combined with the continuity equation, it solves the Venturi tube and the Pitot tube.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

In a Venturi tube, halving the cross-sectional area doubles the speed (continuity), the dynamic pressure rises fourfold, and the pressure drops accordingly; a Pitot tube measures airspeed from the stagnation-point pressure difference.

Limits of validity

Inviscid, incompressible, steady flow along one and the same streamline; turbulence and compressible flow require corrections.

Transfer and counterexample

Is "the upper surface is faster, so the pressure is lower" sufficient to explain lift on an airfoil?

Compare with the transfer-exercise solution

Not entirely; circulation and the Kutta condition give a more complete description of lift, and the simplified statement is often misused.

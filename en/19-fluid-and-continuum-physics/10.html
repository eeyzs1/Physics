Multiphase flow and interfacial instabilities

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the surface-tension lesson; the boundary-layer lesson. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

Gas–liquid, liquid–liquid, and particle-laden flows are full of interfaces: Rayleigh–Taylor (heavy liquid on top) and Kelvin–Helmholtz (velocity shear) instabilities, bubble and droplet dynamics, cavitation and boiling heat transfer — the main battleground of engineering flows.

RT growth rate σ=√(A·gk); KH set by the competition of shear and surface tension

From assumptions to results

  1. A density difference plus gravity gives the RT instability (Atwood number A), with short waves growing fastest (cut off by surface tension).
  2. Velocity shear gives the KH instability (wavy clouds, sea-surface waves).
  3. The Rayleigh–Plateau instability breaks a jet into droplets (droplet radius ≈1.89 times the jet radius).
  4. Bubbles in tubes take the form of Taylor bubbles in slug flow.
  5. Cavitation nuclei grow at low pressure, and their collapse releases microjets (erosion of ship propellers).

Worked example and check

The mushroom cloud of a nuclear blast and supernovae (a link to astrophysics) are both RT; inkjet droplet generation exploits the Plateau instability; in boiling heat transfer the critical heat flux and the bubble-departure frequency are set by interfacial dynamics.

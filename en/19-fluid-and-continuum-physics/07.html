Deriving the Navier–Stokes equations: from forces to flow

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: Newton's second law; the concept of viscosity. If a prerequisite is unfamiliar, first go back to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this branch do not by themselves satisfy every advanced prerequisite.

The problem and the physical picture

Writing m a=F for a fluid element and splitting the forces into pressure, viscous, and body forces yields the Navier–Stokes equations — the hub of continuum mechanics and at the same time a famous unsolved problem (smoothness and uniqueness).

ρ(∂v/∂t+v·∇v)=−∇p+μ∇²v+ρg

From assumptions to results

  1. Write the momentum balance for a fixed fluid element.
  2. Decompose the surface forces into normal pressure and tangential viscous stresses; for a Newtonian fluid the stress is proportional to the rate of strain (coefficient μ).
  3. Substituting gives the NS equations.
  4. Nondimensionalization yields the Reynolds number: for Re≪1 the equations reduce to Stokes flow (linear), while for Re≫1 inertia dominates and the flow moves toward turbulence.

Fill in the intermediate algebra on paper. If you cannot reproduce a step, first pinpoint the equation where you get stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

A feel for the scales: water has μ=10⁻³ Pa·s, honey about 10 Pa·s — four orders of magnitude apart; in a pipe Re=vD/ν, the laminar pressure drop is analytic (Poiseuille), while the turbulent pressure drop can only be handled empirically or numerically; the existence and smoothness of the equations' solutions is one of the Clay Millennium Problems.

Limits of validity

The continuum hypothesis (mean free path ≪ body scale); rarefied flows and turbulence closure remain two wide-open directions.

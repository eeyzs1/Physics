Undergraduate core · about 30–60 min

Plasma diagnostics: probes, interferometry, and scattering

Goal: build the model by following the derivation, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the Debye-shielding lesson; the wave-propagation lesson. If a prerequisite is shaky, first return to Mathematics preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not by themselves satisfy all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

A plasma is opaque to conventional instruments, so it is "seen" through its electromagnetic response: Langmuir probes measure the local density and temperature, interferometers measure the density integrated along a path, and Thomson scattering gives the local temperature/density — diagnostic design is itself plasma physics.

Probe I–V characteristic; Δφ∝∫n_e dl

From assumptions to result

  1. The electron/ion saturation currents and the floating potential of a Langmuir probe give n and T_e.
  2. In microwave/laser interferometry the refractive index n_r≈√(1−n_e/n_c) produces a phase shift proportional to the line-integrated density; tomographic inversion recovers the profile.
  3. In laser Thomson scattering the spectral width ∝√(T_e/m_e) and the intensity ∝n_e.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. When you cannot reproduce a step, first point out the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

Tokamaks monitor recycling with the D-α line, measure the ion temperature by charge-exchange spectroscopy, and measure the electron-temperature profile by ECE; ITER's diagnostic windows must withstand the neutron flux and the heat load simultaneously — diagnostic engineering is itself a device limit.

Limits of validity

Probes perturb the plasma (restricted to low-temperature/edge regions); high densities require shorter-wavelength probe light.

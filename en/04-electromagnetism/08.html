Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Maxwell's equations and the displacement current

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: Gauss's law; Ampère's law; Faraday induction. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics Preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

Writing the four experimental laws of electricity and magnetism as a set of partial differential equations and adding the displacement current, the wave equation emerges naturally — electromagnetic waves are solutions of this system of equations, not an extra assumption.

∇·E=ρ/ε₀; ∇·B=0; ∇×E=−∂B/∂t; ∇×B=μ₀J+μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t

From assumptions to results

  1. Applying Ampère's law to a charging-capacitor circuit exposes a surface-dependence contradiction.
  2. Adding the μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t term makes the curl equation consistent with charge conservation.
  3. Combining the two curl equations and eliminating B gives ∇²E=μ₀ε₀∂²E/∂t².

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

The wave equation gives the speed of light c=1/√(μ₀ε₀)≈3.0×10⁸ m/s, consistent with measurement; on this basis Maxwell declared light to be an electromagnetic wave.

Limits of applicability

Classical theory and macroscopic media (extended through constitutive relations); microscopic and quantum situations require quantum electrodynamics.

Transfer and counterexample

Is the displacement current a current?

Check the transfer-exercise solution

No — it is a changing electric field, not a flow of charge, but it produces a magnetic field just the same and keeps the system of equations self-consistent.

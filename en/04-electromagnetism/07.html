Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Faraday induction and Lenz's law

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: magnetic flux; conservation of energy. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics Preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

A changing magnetic flux drives an EMF in a loop; the minus sign says the induced effect always opposes the change in flux, which is precisely how energy conservation shows up.

ε=−dΦ/dt; Φ=∫B·dA

From assumptions to results

  1. Work done by the Lorentz force on the charges in a moving loop gives the motional EMF.
  2. A changing magnetic field produces an induced electric field, described by ∮E·dl=−dΦ/dt.
  3. The two mechanisms unify into Faraday's law.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point to the equation where you got stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

A 10-turn coil of area 0.01 m², with B dropping linearly from 0.5 T to zero in 0.2 s: ε=10×0.5×0.01/0.2=0.25 V; with a loop resistance of 5 Ω the induced current is 0.05 A.

Limits of applicability

The loop resistance cannot be zero, or the current diverges; at high frequencies, stray capacitance and radiation must be included.

Independent calculation

Show solution

ε∝N, 0.25×2=0.5 V.

Transfer and counterexample

When a magnet is inserted into a coil, what is the direction of the magnetic field produced by the induced current?

Check the transfer-exercise solution

It opposes the increase in flux, that is, it points opposite to the magnet's field (Lenz's law).

Advanced self-test

One more step than the previous problem, or a unit conversion is needed; switch to this problem when reviewing on days 1, 3, and 7 to avoid memorizing answers.

Show solution

|ε|=ΔΦ/Δt=0.1×0.01/0.1=0.01 V=10 mV.

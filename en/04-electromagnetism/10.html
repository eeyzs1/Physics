Magnetic vector potential and gauge

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, compute a new case independently, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: the electromagnetic induction lesson; vector analysis. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, first return to Mathematics Preparation. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

The problem and the physical picture

The magnetic field can be written as the curl of a vector potential, B=∇×A, which makes ∇·B=0 automatic and introduces gauge freedom; in quantum mechanics the particle's phase accumulates the vector potential along its path, and the Aharonov–Bohm effect shows that the vector potential is not a mere mathematical convenience.

B=∇×A; E=−∇φ−∂A/∂t

From assumptions to results

  1. The general solution of ∇·B=0 is written B=∇×A.
  2. Substituting into Faraday's law gives E+∂A/∂t=−∇φ.
  3. The gauge transformations A→A+∇χ and φ→φ−∂χ/∂t leave the fields unchanged.
  4. The quantum path phase ∮A·dl/ℏ shifts the electron interference fringes when the path encloses magnetic flux — the AB effect.

Worked example and check

Outside a long straight solenoid, A_φ=Φ/(2πr): outside the winding B=0 yet ∮A·dl=Φ; when electron double slits enclose the flux the phase difference is 2πΦ/Φ₀, and the flux quantum Φ₀=h/e≈4.1×10⁻¹⁵ Wb is the origin of flux quantization in superconductors.

Limits of applicability

The loop integral of A is gauge invariant, while A itself depends on the gauge; classical mechanics senses only the fields.

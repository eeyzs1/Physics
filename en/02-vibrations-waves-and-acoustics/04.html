Undergraduate core · About 30–60 min

Sound intensity, decibels, and the Doppler effect

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new situation, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: mechanical waves; logarithms. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

Sound intensity is measured in W/m²; decibels use logarithms to compress an enormous dynamic range; when the source or the observer moves, the received frequency shifts according to the Doppler formula, on which radar and medical ultrasound both rely.

β=10 log₁₀(I/I₀)，I₀=10⁻¹² W/m²；f′=f(v∓v₀)/(v∓v_s)

From assumptions to results

  1. Take the logarithm of the intensity ratio to compress the range.
  2. Source motion changes the wavelength, observer motion changes the rate at which wavefronts are met; the two cases unify into the Doppler formula.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

I=10⁻⁶ W/m² corresponds to 60 dB (conversation); an ambulance approaching at 30 m/s (speed of sound 340 m/s): f′=f×340/(340−30)≈1.10f, dropping abruptly after it passes.

Range of applicability

Far field, linear medium; relativistic Doppler shifts and ultrasound focusing require separate corrections.

Independent calculation

Show solution

The ratio is 100, and 10log₁₀100=20 dB.

Transfer and counterexample

An observer approaches a stationary source at 30 m/s — what is f′/f?

Transfer-exercise solution for comparison

(340+30)/340≈1.088.

Advanced self-test

This one adds a step or requires a unit conversion; on your day 1, 3, and 7 reviews, do this problem instead of the previous one to avoid memorizing the answer.

Show solution

The dB increase is 10×lg(100)=20.

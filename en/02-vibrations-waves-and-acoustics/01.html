Understanding vibration through a spring

Lesson goal: be able to explain "understanding vibration through a spring" in your own words, and independently complete the calculations and validity-condition judgments below.

Prerequisites: classical mechanics; trigonometric functions. If the formulas are unclear for now, read the examples first, then return to Learning Preparation.

Start with one question

If the mass becomes 9 times its original value, by what factor does the period change? Note down your intuitive answer first; after reading, compare which step of your thinking changed.

Understanding the model

Pull the object slightly away from equilibrium, and the spring exerts a restoring force toward the equilibrium position. In the ideal undamped case, the displacement varies sinusoidally in time. The amplitude is the maximum deviation; the period is the time for one repetition — the two are not the same quantity.

T = 2π√(m/k)

Order of use: identify the system and its conditions → make the units consistent → write the equation → substitute → check the units and order of magnitude of the result. The precise meanings and conventions of the variables are given in the worked example below.

Work through the example with reasoning

With a mass of 1 kg and a spring constant of 100 N/m, the period is about 0.628 s. Increase the mass to 4 kg, and the period becomes about 1.257 s; within the ideal linear range, increasing the amplitude does not change the period.

Check the boundary cases

Resonance does not mean the amplitude becomes infinite; real systems have damping and nonlinearity.

Look back at the example: name at least one neglected factor, and explain under what circumstances it could affect the result. After changing one condition, does the original formula still apply?

Advanced topic · About 30–60 min

Normal modes: how two pendulums share energy

Goal: follow the derivation to build the model, independently compute a new situation, and point out when the approximation fails.

Prerequisites: matrix eigenvalues; simple harmonic motion. If the prerequisites are unfamiliar, go back to Mathematics preparation first. The two introductory lessons in this field do not amount to meeting all advanced prerequisites.

Problem and physical picture

Described in arbitrary coordinates, the equations of coupled oscillators are entangled with one another. Once normal coordinates are chosen, the linear system splits into independent oscillations; the in-phase and out-of-phase modes have different restoring forces, and hence different frequencies.

mẍ₁=−(k+κ)x₁+κx₂；ω_in²=k/m，ω_out²=(k+2κ)/m

From assumptions to results

  1. Substitute x₁=x₂ and x₁=−x₂ respectively.
  2. In the former, the coupling spring is not stretched; in the latter, the relative displacement is 2x₁.
  3. Two independent frequencies result.

Fill in the intermediate algebraic steps on paper. If you cannot reproduce them, first point to the equation where you are stuck, then ask me.

Worked example and check

With m=1, k=4, κ=2, the in-phase angular frequency is 2 rad/s and the out-of-phase angular frequency is √8 rad/s. A general initial state is a superposition of the two modes.

Range of applicability

The two masses are equal and the motion stays within the linear range; with nonlinear coupling, the modes may exchange energy.

Transfer and counterexample

Why does pulling aside only one oscillator lead to energy sloshing back and forth?

Transfer-exercise solution for comparison

This initial state excites both modes, whose frequencies differ slightly, at once; their superposition produces beats, which appear in the physical coordinates as an exchange of energy.
